## When to Use

Use this when you are a leader who finds yourself solving every problem for your team, when your direct reports keep bringing you their monkeys, or when you want to develop people rather than just direct them. Rachel Lockett, an executive coach and former HR leader at Stripe and Pinterest, identifies this as the single biggest gap in technical leaders: "Most leaders, especially technical leaders, assume they have to have all the answers. People have climbed the ladder because they've been dependable, reliable, the smartest person in the room. But great leaders know that when you try to advise and have the answer all the time, you're not actually equipping your team to go solve the hard problems. You're training your team to come to you with all of the hard problems."

## The Framework

The framework has two foundational skills and one structured model that ties them together.

### Skill 1: Active Listening (Three Levels)

Lockett describes three levels of listening, drawn from coaching methodology:

**Level 1 — Internal listening**: "You're talking to me about a problem. I'm thinking about the implications of that problem on me. I'm completely distracted with my own inner dialogue." This is where most rushed leaders operate. As she quotes from Fight Club: "Most people aren't listening. They're just waiting for their turn to talk."

**Level 2 — Focused listening**: "You're talking to me, and I can repeat back what you're saying." This is the standard in a decent one-on-one. You are tracking the words.

**Level 3 — Global listening**: "That's what I'm hearing beneath the words. I'm hearing what you're communicating, not just what you're saying. I see your body language. I notice your tone of voice. I know the context around what you're talking about, and I can reflect back more insight about what's happening than you're aware of."

Lockett demonstrates this live on the podcast by asking Lenny about being a father and then reflecting back: "Initially, I saw you really squirm in your chair... You looked up and down and kind of avoided my eye contact at first because my sense is you love being a dad, and it's so challenging. It's so tiring. And I'm hearing both of that in your answer."

Lenny's response: "It feels really nice to be heard. And it's not just like you're repeating back my words. It's here's what I got out of the level below what you're saying."

The key point: "Active listening doesn't mean you're setting up an hour coaching session with every person on your team. No one has time for that. But even in the time you're already spending, just focusing on the other person in a way that is novel and really gives them your full attention... goes a long way."

### Skill 2: Powerful Questions

"Asking powerful questions means I'm curious about what's really going on, and there's not one right answer. A powerful question helps you gain insight and it takes you to a new solution set you didn't have before. But it's not me leading the witness. I'm not trying to guide you to a specific answer."

This is where the GROW model provides structure.

### The GROW Model

Four categories of questions. They do not need to be asked in order.

| Letter | Category | Sample Questions |
|--------|----------|-----------------|
| **G** — Goal | What does success look like? | "What's the outcome that you want to have?" / "What does dream state look like?" |
| **R** — Reality | Where are you stuck right now? | "Where are you stuck? What are your current challenges? What have you tried?" |
| **O** — Options | What paths could you take? | "What are the various paths you could take?" / "What are the things you could do?" |
| **W** — Way Forward | What will you do next? | "What's one thing you could do in the next two weeks?" |

Lockett clarifies: "You don't have to do it in this order. These are just four kinds of questions. You might come and someone's super clear about their outcome. You know that. You don't need to spend any time asking them questions about that. Maybe you just want to really dig in on where are they stuck?"

### When to Coach vs. When to Advise

Not every situation calls for coaching. Advise when:
- There is an urgent issue and you have a clear answer you want to drive
- The person does not have the skill they need
- You have something you absolutely want them to do

Lockett warns: "Don't coach and make it a game. You want your person on your team to guess what's in your mind. That's not a good time to coach."

Coach when the person has the right context and can solve their own problem. "Most leaders over-index on the advising solution."

### When They Reach a Bad Conclusion

If their answer concerns you, stay curious: "Help me understand how you came to that conclusion because here's my reaction to that." You are honest but still creating space. "Coaching in a manager or a leader context is not the same as in an executive coaching conversation. You're managing this person. You're responsible for their outcomes."

## Example

Lockett coaches Lenny live on the podcast through the GROW model. His challenge: feeling overwhelmed by endless work, the "Indiana Jones boulder" always chasing him.

- **G (Goal)**: She asks what dream state looks like. Lenny says: "25% more free time while everything else continues to be awesome."
- **R (Reality)**: She explores his current patterns. He realizes: "I sometimes get that extra time and then I fill it with more projects and opportunities. That's the problem right there."
- **O (Options)**: He identifies reducing newsletter cadence, bringing on more help, and revisiting his policies on what he says yes to.
- **W (Way Forward)**: He commits to skipping a newsletter week and revisiting his no-list.

Then Lockett names the meta-lesson: "Typically when you're coached versus told what to do, you're more bought in. If I told you, Lenny, here's what you should do... I could have given you a laundry list of things that I thought you should do without much context. But you're the expert on your own context and actually what resonates. And you're much more likely to do it if you came up with it."

She also shares the story of a client, an AI company founder who was the blocker on every decision. Through coaching, he created squads, shifted from deciding everything to asking good questions in check-ins, and aligned to KPIs. The result: "The squads are moving so much faster. The teams feel more empowered and motivated." And he had time to "pick his head up and plan for 2026, and spend his time and his gifts. Which are product, vision and strategy."

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:
- An awareness of which listening level you default to (probably level 1 or 2) and a practice for reaching level 3
- The GROW acronym as a cheat sheet for your next one-on-one where someone brings you a problem
- A clear decision rule for when to coach (they have context) vs. when to advise (they need your knowledge or it is urgent)
- A 15% shift: not an hour-long coaching session, just more open-ended questions in the time you already spend
