## When to Use

Use this framework when you are in conflict with a leader, a peer, or another team and the disagreement feels irrational — where both sides are smart and well-intentioned but cannot reach alignment. It is also useful for diagnosing organizational culture: understanding what behavior your company actually rewards, regardless of what it says it rewards. If you have ever left a product review wondering "why couldn't the CEO see my perfectly rational argument," this framework explains what happened.

## The Framework

All product work operates at one of three levels. Most conflict between smart, well-intentioned people happens because they are defaulting to different levels without realizing it.

> "This idea that there are three levels of product work, impact, execution, and optics. Once you understand it, it explains a lot of what you see on product teams and organizations in general."

### Level 1: Impact

Working at the impact level means asking: what is the outcome for the customer, the business, or the brand? CEOs and founders tend to default here.

Shreyas describes the classic mismatch: a PM presents a launch plan to the CEO. The CEO asks why customer service response times will be poor. The PM has a perfectly rational execution-level answer — the VP of support does not have funding this quarter, but will next quarter. The CEO is not satisfied.

> "The reason is that you are thinking at different levels. So you as a PM perhaps are fixated... on the execution level, which is what does it take to get something done?... The CEO on the other hand is approaching it from the impact level. And particularly perhaps in this case, what is the impact to the customer experience?"

### Level 2: Execution

Working at the execution level means asking: what does it take to get this done? How do we hit the next milestone? PMs in the thick of a launch tend to default here.

> "You as a PM perhaps are fixated as you are dealing with this launch or this project, you are fixated on the execution level, which is what does it take to get something done? And how can I do it? How can I hit the next milestone?"

### Level 3: Optics

Working at the optics level means creating awareness of the impact and execution your team is doing. Internal optics — status updates, launch announcements, all-hands presentations.

Shreyas is careful to note that optics is not inherently bad:

> "Optics is about creating awareness of the impact and the execution that you're doing or your team is doing... optics is a good thing... it creates energy, it creates awareness, it creates excitement, it creates opportunities for feedback."

The danger is when optics becomes the goal:

> "The challenge with optics is that in certain organizations that balance gets thrown off, where optics sometimes becomes the goal where somehow implicitly the organization or its culture has indicated to its people that as long as you do the optics well, you are going to be fine, you are going to be appreciated here, you're going to be rewarded here."

### Step 1: Identify Your Default Level

Most people have a default level they operate at. This is not fixed — you can switch — but it requires a conscious nudge. When you are in conflict with someone, ask: are we simply defaulting to different levels?

> "We litigate the minutiae of whatever issue we are discussing, but we never really recognize that it's because we are default thinking at different levels."

### Step 2: Identify Your Organization's Default Level

Look at what your company actually rewards. Who gets promoted? What gets celebrated at all-hands?

> "It's not like the organization woke up in the morning and said, 'This is the culture we want to create.' It just happens... through who you hire, who you fire, who you promote and what kinds of things do you appreciate at all hands... a status update is an optics activity. Now, it is a necessary optics activity, but if you start appreciating the necessary optics activities constantly, the signal you are sending to people is, 'Oh, you got to focus on this optics activity.'"

### Step 3: Choose the Right Level for Your Context

The right level depends on your situation. There is no universally correct answer.

> "The point is not so much like, 'Oh, this is the right level and all other levels are wrong,' it's about being sensitive to what's right in this situation."

### Step 4: Make It Explicit with Your Team

Use this vocabulary to have an objective conversation about which level matters most right now, without pointing fingers.

> "Now we have a vocabulary that we can talk about in an objective manner without pointing fingers."

## Example

Shreyas gives two concrete examples of how different teams should choose different default levels:

**Early-stage startup — default to execution.** "For early stage teams, of course, they need to be thinking about the eventual impact, but what they should actually, I think, most early stage teams should actually optimize for execution. Assuming that they have come up with a reasonable hypothesis about what's going to win, their main emphasis needs to be on execution because you will not see impact readily on a one week horizon or a one month horizon."

**Platform team with reliability issues — elevate optics.** "Say there is a platform team and that platform team has had some issues lately with availability that has disrupted some other teams... Perhaps that platform team should have a conversation that, 'You know what, yes, we need to focus on impact obviously to avoid this negative impact, but also let's pay some more attention to optics because we haven't been communicating with teams as much.'"

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **A diagnosis of your current conflict** — which levels are the two sides defaulting to, and how to bridge the gap
2. **An explicit team agreement** on which level matters most given your current context
3. **A cultural audit** — does your organization reward impact, execution, or optics? Is that what you want?
4. **A shared vocabulary** for raising level-mismatch issues without making it personal
5. **Context-specific guidance** for your team: "For the next quarter, we are optimizing for execution. Impact thinking informed our strategy. Optics gets 10% of our attention."
