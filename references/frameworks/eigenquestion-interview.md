## When to Use

Use this when your team is stuck in a cycle of debating the same set of hard questions meeting after meeting, or when you need to evaluate a candidate's ability to think from first principles. Shishir Mehrotra, co-founder and CEO of Coda and former VP of Product and Engineering at YouTube, developed the eigenquestion concept at YouTube in 2008 and has used it as both a strategic decision-making tool and an interview technique ever since. The concept: "The eigenquestion is the question that when answered also answers the most subsequent questions."

## The Framework

### Part 1: Finding the Eigenquestion

The method is deceptively simple:

1. List all the questions your team is debating
2. Do not rank them by importance or urgency
3. Instead, rank them by which one, if answered, would eliminate the most other questions on the list

Mehrotra explains: "We tend to only practice it in real world scenarios that are high stakes... one of the things I encourage people to do is to practice eigenquestion in completely almost frivolous situations."

The name comes from linear algebra. "Eigenvectors are in a multidimensional space. They're the most discriminating vectors of the vectors in that space." But as Mehrotra notes, "the math doesn't really matter."

### Part 2: The YouTube Origin Story — Consistency vs. Comprehensiveness

In 2008, YouTube was debating whether to link out to ABC.com when users searched for Modern Family, the most popular show on television. The company split:

- Product team: "That's what the user wants, link them off to ABC.com. We're not owned by Google, Google tries hard to prioritize, do right by the user."
- Business team: "Please, please, please don't do that. If you do that and you start linking off to all these other places, nobody's ever going to put good content on YouTube."

Mehrotra describes the deadlock: "These are almost impossible to solve problems. And this would happen meeting after meeting after meeting, did we make a decision yet? Modern Family, what are we going to do?"

The night before a make-or-break offsite, Mehrotra read about the Google Shopping team's fight with Amazon. Users were telling Google: "I picked Amazon because I value consistency over comprehensiveness." He reframed YouTube's debate around one eigenquestion: **In a decade from now, is the online video market more likely to be about consistency or about comprehensiveness?**

"We basically have this discussion and we all came to the conclusion this market is going to value consistency over comprehensiveness. And by the way, I think, now almost 15 years later, I think we were right."

Once that was answered, everything else fell into place:
- Link out to ABC.com? No.
- Embed other players on YouTube? Stop doing that.
- Let Apple build and own the YouTube iPhone app? Take it back.

On the Apple decision, Mehrotra drove to Cupertino and told them: "We value consistency over comprehensiveness. We would much rather be on fewer phones with a more consistent experience than be on all of them with an inconsistent experience." Apple was not happy — "You're going to have to start from zero. We're not giving you a single download for free" — but within six months YouTube had 80% share on iPhone.

### Part 3: The Teleportation Device Interview Question

Mehrotra uses a low-stakes version to test eigenquestion thinking in interviews:

"A group of scientists have invented a teleportation device. They've hired you to be their business counterpart, bring this to market. What do you do?"

Candidates generate a long list of questions: Is it big? Small? Safe? Does it need a receiver? Then the twist: "These scientists, they kind of hate talking to people and they're kind of annoyed by all your questions. They've decided that they will answer only two of your questions and after that they expect a plan. What two questions do you ask?"

Mehrotra's favorite answer came from a PM who asked:
1. **Is it safe enough for humans or not?**
2. **Is it more expensive to buy them or to run them (CapEx vs. OpEx)?**

With just those two questions, the PM mapped out four quadrants of possible businesses: human fax machines (safe, cheap to buy, expensive to run), airport replacements (safe, expensive to buy, cheap to run), high-value goods transport (not safe for humans, optimize for expensive cargo), and so on.

Mehrotra notes: "If you ask a kid that question, almost every kid will quickly get to two pretty good eigenquestions. It's actually a skill we remove from ourselves."

What he watches for in poor answers: "I'll hear candidates tell me things like, 'Well, I guess I would ask them what size it is.' And they're like, 'Why would you ask them what size, what decision is that going to allow you to make, to know what size it is?' And sometimes I can explain it, but sometimes not, don't get hired."

### Part 4: Reference Checks over Interviews

Mehrotra considers eigenquestion thinking part of a broader talent evaluation framework he calls PSHE (Problem, Solution, How, Execution). He values the P-level thinker — the person who identifies the right problems — most highly, and notes: "Being a great P thinker is very correlated with being good with eigenquestions."

But his strongest signal comes from reference checks, not interviews: "I generally value the reference check over interview signals. If I had to stack rank in interviews, what is the best signal? The reference check is the top of the list. Those people, they worked with this person sometimes for years, their knowledge, what you're going to get out of 30 minutes of artificial scenarios is just like never going to compare what a good reference check will give you."

His reference check technique for assessing PSHE level: "When you think about this person, I'll give you four different personas. Someone who's regularly coming up with the problems that the team should be focused on. Someone who given a set of problems is constantly solving them in this creative way. The person that is just really good at getting a team moving. Or the person who can take a playbook and execute it with high precision."

The ideal approach: "You get to the person that you're doing the reference check with and you don't even tell them who you're asking about and you just say, 'When you think of your teams, who is best at...'" This removes the perverse incentives that make most reference checks uninformative.

## Example

Coda's founding eigenquestion: "Are we more committed to being a doc or being an app?" Mehrotra explains: "Coda allows anyone to make a doc as powerful as an app. You can reverse that statement and say, allow anyone to make an app as easily as a doc. And those two sound similar, but they're actually quite different statements." They decided doc-first — and cemented it by naming the company Coda (doc backwards). "I said, 'Well, we're definitely not revisiting this one. Coda is a doc first.'"

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:
- A method for breaking strategic deadlocks: list all debated questions, rank by which eliminates the most others
- A low-stakes exercise (teleportation device or any hypothetical) to train eigenquestion thinking on your team
- An interview question that tests first-principles thinking without requiring domain knowledge
- A reference check protocol (PSHE personas) that reveals whether a candidate identifies problems (P), solves them creatively (S), organizes well (H), or executes reliably (E)
