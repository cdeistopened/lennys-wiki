## When to Use

Use this framework when evaluating a product strategy, feature bet, or competitive response. It is designed to move teams beyond "will users like this?" toward a more complete question: will this delight users in a way that competitors cannot easily replicate, while building a better business? It works for both individual feature decisions and portfolio-level strategy reviews. Gibson Biddle, former VP of Product at Netflix and Chegg, developed it during his time at Netflix under Reed Hastings.

## The Framework

The DHM model asks three questions about any product initiative. All three must be true for a strategy to be durable.

### Question 1: Does It Delight Customers?

The starting point is always the customer. Biddle traces this directly to Reed Hastings:

> "He did a reference check to a friend of mine, checking up on me, and the only question he asked my pal, whose name is John Daz, was, 'Hey, John, is Gib ready? Can Gib delight customers?' That was the only question he asked in the reference check."

Delight is not the same as satisfaction. It means the product creates an emotional response — surprise, joy, relief — that makes customers want to stay and tell others.

### Question 2: Is It Hard to Copy?

Delight alone is insufficient if competitors can replicate it in a week. Biddle illustrates this with a Netflix example from the early days competing with Blockbuster:

> "I'm like, that's great HB, but I'll bet my paycheck that within a week, Blockbuster's going to put a happy family on their couch, too. And this is the problem of doing things that are easy to copy. So in the long term, if you can delight customers and create hard to copy advantage, things are just a lot better."

Hard-to-copy advantages at Netflix included personalization (powered by massive viewing data), original content, and the recommendation algorithm. Each of these required years of investment and proprietary data that competitors could not replicate overnight.

> "What makes Netflix hard to copy?" Lenny answers: "The licensing deals and content that they're creating."

### Question 3: Is It Margin-Enhancing?

The initiative must contribute to building a better business — not just delight users at the company's expense. "Margin enhancement" is Biddle's phrase for making money, broadly defined.

> "The last phrase, margin enhancement, that's just a fancy phrase for making money. Margin technically would mean profit."

Personalization at Netflix serves all three: it delights users by surfacing relevant content, it is hard to copy because it requires Netflix's scale of viewing data, and it is margin-enhancing because it helps Netflix right-size content investments:

> "When Netflix is making an investment in a TV show or movie, they kind of can guess how many people will watch it. So, they guessed that 100 million people would watch Stranger Things, so they were willing to invest $500 million. They guessed that 20 million people would watch BoJack Horseman... I call that right sizing the investment, but that's how personalization helps Netflix to build margin."

### Applying the Model: "DHM It"

For any strategic decision, run it through all three lenses. Biddle walks through this process live with Lenny on several Netflix cases.

**Case: Auto-canceling inactive subscribers.** Netflix CEO Eddie considered auto-canceling users who were paying but not watching.

> "Is this a delightful thing to do?" — Yes. "Is there hard to copy advantage?" — Yes, competitors would not voluntarily give up revenue. "The bad news, you're going to lose a hundred million bucks."

The model makes the trade-off explicit: "There is delight, there is hard to copy advantage, and you're going to lose a hundred million bucks. Does that delight and hard to copy advantage outweigh the negative of losing a hundred million?"

Netflix shipped it. The brand halo and hard-to-copy positioning outweighed the revenue loss.

**Case: Ad-supported tier.**

> "Would an ad-supported plan that's lower price be delightful for customers?"

Not exactly delightful — but it extends access to price-sensitive users. The margin-enhancing argument is straightforward (additional revenue stream). The hard-to-copy dimension is weaker. The model helps identify where to pressure-test the strategy.

### Testing High-Level Hypotheses

Biddle recommends generating ten ideas that might delight in hard-to-copy, margin-enhancing ways, then testing them. Most will fail:

> "We'd have 10 ideas and probably six of them would fail, but a lot of value in the three or the four that worked."

The metric for testing is retention impact. On a specific Netflix experiment with Xbox Party, Biddle estimated the feature would only reach 5% engagement:

> "We actually did Xbox Party back in 2008, 2009. My guess with the Xbox team was it would be a two percenter. I think it barely squeaked to 5%. And then, because it was only at 5%, we killed it."

### The Goal: Stop Caring About Competitors

The ultimate test of a DHM strategy is that you stop looking over your shoulder:

> "You want to feel like you don't have to compete with peeps, really. You don't like it when you're always on guard about what your competitors should do. You sort of want to get to this place where you don't really care, and your sort of true North is okay, how can we best serve our customer?"

## Example

Netflix's personalization algorithm is the textbook DHM example. It delights users by surfacing content they want to watch (reducing browse time). It is hard to copy because it requires Netflix's proprietary viewing data across hundreds of millions of users. And it is margin-enhancing because it allows Netflix to right-size content investments — knowing how many people will watch a show before committing hundreds of millions of dollars.

The password-sharing crackdown is another DHM case Biddle walks through. Roughly 30 million people were using Netflix on someone else's account. The question becomes testing and math:

> "If you get an email saying, hey, we've noticed you're enjoying the service on somebody else's nickel, would you like to upgrade to your own plan? What percent of those folks are going to say yes, right? I mean, do you have a guess on that? I don't have a guess and I don't have an answer. But this is the game, right?"

## Output

After applying the DHM model, you walk away with:

1. **A strategic filter** — for any feature or initiative, a clear yes/no on each of the three dimensions
2. **Identified weaknesses** — if a feature delights but is easy to copy, you know where to invest in differentiation
3. **A prioritization lens** — ideas that score on all three dimensions rise to the top
4. **A vocabulary for trade-offs** — the team can discuss "this is high-delight but margin-negative" in a shared language
5. **A testing agenda** — for each high-level hypothesis, a concrete experiment to measure retention impact
6. **A competitive moat assessment** — which of your advantages are genuinely hard to copy vs. cosmetic

The model's power is its simplicity. Three questions. Every strategy meeting. As Biddle puts it: "We'll DHM it."
