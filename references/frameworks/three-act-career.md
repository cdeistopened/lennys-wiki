## When to Use

Use this framework when you are making a career decision and your reasoning is entirely about the next job — the title, the compensation, the logo. Use it when you feel restless after achieving a major career milestone and cannot explain why. Use it when you are 15 or 20 years into your career and realize the motivation that got you here is not the motivation that will carry you forward. Use it when you are coaching someone who is about to optimize for promotion when they should be thinking about the shape of their entire professional life.

Nikhyl Singhal developed this model through coaching hundreds of product leaders across transitions. He is VP of Product at Meta (overseeing groups, stories, messaging, and the Facebook feed), former CPO at Credit Karma, and a three-time startup co-founder. He writes about career strategy in his newsletter and podcast, The Skip.

## The Framework

### The Core Insight: Careers Are Getting Longer

The framework starts from a demographic reality that most people ignore. Careers are no longer 30 years. They are potentially 60:

> "Health is getting better. You might see folks work until their 70s or 80s. So that means that their careers are potentially 60 years long. So even if you're 20 years or 30 years in your career, you're only halfway through."

This means the standard career arc — climb, peak, retire — is obsolete. You need a model that accounts for what happens after you reach the top of the ladder you set out to climb.

### The Greyhound Problem

Nikhyl uses a vivid analogy from his childhood in the Midwest to describe what happens when high performers achieve their goals without a next act:

> "The way that they motivated the dogs was they had these fake rabbits... Sometimes the machines would break, the dogs would actually catch the rabbit, they would never run again. The reason why they wouldn't run again is because there was like, 'Well, what's next? I've achieved what I was looking for.'"

This is not a theoretical problem. Nikhyl sees it constantly among senior tech leaders:

> "Your listeners are spending time focused on like, 'Well, one day I will be X. I will be that vice president. I will have more money. I will have built something. I will have started a company.' But they don't think about what happens next, and when it happens, when they succeed, their North Star, their entire way of wiring their career, themselves, it has been around getting to that place."

### Act One: Building the Car (Years 0-10)

The early career. You are learning to build. You are developing a craft, picking a lane, and accumulating diverse experiences. Nikhyl recommends becoming world-class in at least one type of product ambiguity:

> "They want to build something that they as much as possible are world-class in... you can be a great crafter. You could be incredibly strong at market ambiguity... You can be great at organizational ambiguity... You can be a domain expert... You can be a team expert."

Key act-one principles:
- **Think long-term, not short-term.** Do not optimize for escaping a bad manager or chasing a title: "Lateral moves are by definition not forward moves."
- **Think about the skip.** Not your boss's job — your boss's boss's job: "I always think about not the next job, but the one after it."
- **Accumulate diverse experiences.** Pre-product-market-fit, growth stage, scale — the more movies you have seen in different frames, the better builder you become.
- **Pick a lane and tell a story.** Your career needs a narrative that a future employer can follow: "Think about the story, think about the skill, then solve your day to day."

### Act Two: Building the Factory (Years 10-25)

The mid-career. You are no longer just building things — you are building the systems and teams that build things. This is when management skills matter, when organizational ambiguity becomes the core challenge, and when most people hit the promotion ceiling.

Nikhyl identifies four reasons people stall in act two:

1. **No advocate sees your magic.** You need someone to champion you. If nobody does, change the setting.
2. **The next role does not exist.** In the post-hypergrowth era, companies are not expanding. The job you deserve may not be open.
3. **Impatience.** Leadership takes longer to absorb than IC skills. Annual promotions are not the norm at senior levels.
4. **A real development area you cannot see.** "About 50% of the cases where it's really, there is a development area but it isn't quite connected to the individual."

The antidote is pulling real feedback — not formal feedback. Nikhyl points to Jules Walter's approach of making people feel safe giving you hard truths: "Jules will look for feedback, then he'll repeat it back to me better than even I presented it."

### Act Three: Giving (Years 25+)

The act most people never plan for — and it could be the longest third of your career. Nikhyl observes that people who succeed in act two often become lost:

> "What often happens is, and this is what I've been watching for people that are at my age, is they sort of succeed and then they become lost. They almost goes hand in hand."

Without a new North Star, the consequences are real: "You'll keep working because you had nothing else to do, but you'll be sadder, or you'll find ways to create war when peace is needed, or you'll spend money in an attempt to earn more, or you'll find habits that are bad."

Nikhyl sees two categories of act-three North Stars:

**Scaled economics:** Making tens into hundreds, investing, private equity. Whether this is a good or bad quest is a personal decision.

**Giving:** Eastern philosophies describe this as the end state of happiness. Nikhyl argues it is okay to take and create in acts one and two — this may be the time on the planet where you need to: "Maybe to be provocative, I think that it's okay for you in act one and act two to not predicate yourself around the notion of giving to others because this is maybe the time on the planet where you need to take and you need to create."

But act three is where giving becomes both possible and fulfilling: "If you can find ways to give, whatever that means to you, however that translates to you, is that content, is that volunteer, is that starting a company that is more mission based... if you are able to do that for 30 years and be giving, not only is that going to be more fulfilling than your act one and act two, but it's tremendous for society."

### Start Planning Act Three Now

The most important implication of the model is that you should not wait until act three to define your act-three North Star:

> "If you're going to get there 30 years in and you have a 60-year career, a lot of the discussion I've been having with myself and with others has been, you probably need to start working on that North Star now."

## Example

Lenny Rachitsky's career illustrates the model in action. Act one: engineer. Act two: founder (startup acquired by Airbnb), then product manager at Airbnb. After selling his company and arriving at Airbnb, Lenny hit the greyhound problem: "I got to Airbnb and I was just like, 'What the hell do I do now? I don't have any other goals.' And it was pretty sad."

Act three: Lenny reinvented himself through his newsletter and podcast — giving through content creation while building a livelihood. Nikhyl frames this as the ideal: "What you did is you said, 'I think the thing that I want to do is give, but I want to do it in my own way and I want to create something, but I want to do something that I think I can do for 30 years.' It has lots and lots of spokes to it. So you reinvented yourself professionally, but you created a new North Star."

Nikhyl's own act-three plan: coaching and giving at scale, using his product skills to solve the distribution problem of mentorship. "I definitively plan to devote my act three towards coaching and giving to others and lifting up those that with the right advice at the right time can change their trajectory. But scaling that and doing that in a way that is very authentic is really the hardest part and it's product problem."

## Output

After applying the Three-Act Career Model, you walk away with:

1. **A diagnosis of which act you are in** — and whether your current decisions serve this act or a future one
2. **A skip-level career target** — not the next job, but the job after that, and what this job needs to teach you
3. **A North Star for act three** — even if it is rough, having one prevents the greyhound problem
4. **A career diversity audit** — have you seen enough different movies (pre-PMF, growth, scale, consumer, B2B) to be a versatile builder
5. **Permission to think in decades** — the framework reframes career anxiety from "am I behind?" to "I have 30 more years to build something meaningful"
