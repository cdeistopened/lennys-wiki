## When to Use

Use the JTBD Buying Timeline when your sales process is built around how you want to sell rather than how customers actually buy. It applies when conversion rates are stuck despite a strong product, when your demo-to-close rate is low, or when you are designing onboarding and retention flows. Moesta frames the core problem: "I wrote a book called Demand Side Sales that basically took the premise of stop trying to sell people and just help them make progress, help them buy. The whole book is instead of trying to base the sales process on how we want to sell, we need to actually design the sales process on how they want to buy."

## The Framework

The Buying Timeline maps six phases that every customer moves through when making a switch. Most companies only design for the middle (active looking and deciding) and ignore the rest. Moesta names them directly: "There's actually, I call them six phases. First thought, passive looking, active looking, deciding, first use, and then ongoing use. How do we build the new habit?"

### Phase 1: First Thought

Something happens in the customer's life that creates a push — a struggling moment. This is the seed of demand, and it has nothing to do with your product. Moesta explains: "A struggling moment causes demand. And you start to realize that in some cases that struggling moment exists and can exist for a long time and nobody solved it."

The customer may not even be aware they have a problem worth solving. Your product is invisible to them at this stage.

### Phase 2: Passive Looking

The customer is now problem-aware but solution-unaware. They are not actively searching — they are noticing things, reading articles, hearing about alternatives in passing. Moesta describes this as the phase where people need to learn: "There's something called passive looking where they're problem aware and solution unaware and they have to learn a bunch of things."

This is where content marketing, word of mouth, and education matter most. Pushing a demo at this stage is counterproductive.

### Phase 3: Active Looking

The customer is now both problem-aware and solution-aware. They are comparing options, framing criteria, and building a shortlist. Moesta distinguishes this from passive looking: "Then there's active looking where they're both problem and solution aware and they're trying to figure it out and frame a solution."

### Phase 4: Deciding

The customer is making trade-offs between specific options. This is the only phase where traditional sales closing techniques apply. Moesta notes the phase is "about making trade-offs."

### Phase 5: First Use

The purchase is made, but the job is not done. The customer must successfully adopt the product. This is where onboarding design matters. If first use is painful, the customer churns regardless of how strong the purchase decision was.

### Phase 6: Ongoing Use

The customer builds a new habit around the product. Moesta frames this as the completion of the switch: "How do we build the new habit?" If the product does not become habitual, the customer remains vulnerable to the next competitor.

### The Critical Insight: Meet Customers Where They Are

Moesta illustrates the cost of ignoring the timeline with a company called Autobooks, which helps banks offer invoicing through Apple Pay. Their old process funneled everyone toward a demo and then tried to close. The problem: "What if they're actually in passive looking and want a demo to learn more? It's very different than if I'm trying to close."

The fix was to ask customers where they were in their buying process and serve three different demos: "One about telling stories and giving them the background about the problem, another one about showing them all the alternatives, and then the last one is about basically giving them choices between ways to move forward."

The result: "It actually made the sales process almost half and at 4x basically conversion because now we meet them where they are as opposed to where we want them to be."

### Interviewing for the Timeline

To map the timeline for your product, Moesta interviews people who recently purchased — not people who want to purchase. The distinction matters enormously: "If I talk to people who want to buy a house, they tell me they want granite and hardwood and they'll make everything these things they want. But when you actually talk to people who bought a house, they actually made a lot of trade-offs."

He recommends 10 to 12 interviews: "From a causal mechanism perspective and from a set theory perspective, meaning the sets of pushes, pulls, anxieties and habits, it starts to repeat around seven or eight. And I usually do 10, no more than 12."

The method is not a survey — it is investigative storytelling: "The method itself is not based on traditional research or market research, asking people what it is. It's actually based on criminal and intelligence interrogation about telling me the story about how you decided today's the day I bought a house or today's the day I went back to school."

## Example

Autobooks, a fintech company based in Detroit that helps banks offer invoicing, restructured their entire sales process around the buying timeline. Before, they had one demo designed to close. After mapping the timeline, they created three distinct demo experiences matched to passive looking, active looking, and deciding. The sales cycle shortened by half and conversion quadrupled.

Moesta also shares an example from housing: a customer who bought a $137 coat rack described it as a one-week decision. When Moesta unpacked the full timeline, the actual buying process spanned 18 months of passive and active looking before the purchase. "In their mind, they say they bought it in a week, but the reality is the debate about getting it and why they couldn't get it was happening for over 18 months."

## Output

After mapping the JTBD Buying Timeline for your product, you walk away with:

1. **A six-phase map** of how your specific customers actually buy, with the real duration and triggers for each phase
2. **Tailored content and touchpoints** for each phase — education for passive looking, comparison tools for active looking, trade-off assistance for deciding
3. **A redesigned sales or onboarding process** that meets customers where they are instead of where you want them to be
4. **Churn insights** — studying people who left your product reveals the struggling moments and timeline that led to their switch
5. **A foundation for the Forces of Progress** — each phase of the timeline has its own balance of pushes, pulls, anxieties, and habits
