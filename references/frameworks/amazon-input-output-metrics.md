## When to Use

Use Input/Output Metrics when your team spends the last month of every quarter scrambling to hit revenue targets with promotions and discounts that pull forward demand but create no lasting value. When the response to missing a number is "send another email" or "run a flash sale." When you realize, as Amazon did, that "these fire drills don't really work. We didn't really get meaningful progress against the number with these last-minute things we decided to go do. And oh, by the way, they were a big distraction." This framework is for any team that wants to shift from reactive short-term thinking to building the durable customer experience improvements that actually drive long-term growth.

## The Framework

The Input/Output Metrics framework separates the metrics your business tracks into two categories: outputs you want to achieve (revenue, free cash flow, active customers) and inputs you can directly improve (selection, price, delivery speed, customer experience quality). The core principle is simple: obsess over the inputs. Take it on faith that if you improve them, the outputs follow.

### Step 1: Identify Your Growth Flywheel

Amazon's turning point came when the leadership team read Good to Great by Jim Collins. Carr considers it "the single most influential and effective management book for our company" and "the best, most important management book you'll ever read." It helped Amazon codify its growth flywheel — the reinforcing loop of inputs that drive the business. For Amazon, these were: broad selection, great customer experience (easy to find, easy to buy, fast delivery), low prices, many merchants on the platform, and low cost structure.

"This identification of these things was such a critical moment for the company because then it realized, 'Okay. What we need to do is spend our time focusing on how do I measure each one of those things, and then how do I improve each one of those things?'"

### Step 2: Define Input Metrics for Each Flywheel Element

Map your end-to-end customer experience and instrument every step. Carr walks through how he would apply this to Airbnb: "Step one is that they clicked on some ad somewhere and showed up in the website or the app. Now you're in the app. Now you're looking at this first screen. Well, the first thing, what they're doing is they're browsing and/or they're searching. Okay. How are we measuring the speed, quality, and ease of that browsing and searching? Now they've got onto a detail page for an individual property. How are we measuring the speed, ease, and quality of the different actions they may take?"

Three tests for whether you have a good input metric:

1. **You control it** — Can you apply resources to make it better or worse?
2. **It touches customers** — Is it measuring something in the customer experience?
3. **It is causal, not just correlated** — Does improving it actually move the output? "Which ones are the right metrics, which ones are the most causal to the outputs, I couldn't begin to tell you. This is actually what you're getting paid for."

### Step 3: Goal-Set and Review Against Inputs, Not Outputs

At Amazon, the S-Team maintained a list of roughly 500 goals. Carr reveals a striking detail: "They looked at that list, which is about 500 items long by the way, and they counted it up. And of that list, only 10 of them actually had a financial metric in it, like revenue or free cash flow or gross profit." The other 490+ were input metrics — things like selection breadth, price competitiveness, and delivery speed.

Weekly business reviews focused on input metrics. Operating plans were built around improving inputs. "We took it as an article of faith that if we can just improve these inputs, the outputs will take care of themselves."

### Step 4: Iterate on Measurement Itself

Your first attempt at measuring an input will likely be wrong. Carr shares that Amazon spent years measuring selection incorrectly: "We tell a story in the book about one of our most important input metrics, which was how much selection do we have, and we were actually not measuring that right for several years. We had to refine that measurement."

The process borrows from Six Sigma's DMAIC methodology: Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control. "We just use that process... And by the way, the way we think about it first is like, 'Well, you need to throw a lot of things at the wall. You don't really know which of these things are going to be the most causal.'"

## Example

In the late 1990s and early 2000s, Amazon was a public company hitting growth walls. The pattern was familiar to any product team: "Growth started to... It wasn't just all up into the right. Every company's going to hit a wall eventually." Each quarter they were not going to hit their revenue number, "what ensues then is, 'We're not going to hit our number. What should we do about that?' And this often happens with, well, there's a month or a month and a half left in the quarter, and then we would run around like chickens with our heads cut off and come up with a bunch of ideas that tended to be promotional in nature and tended to be price reduction in nature."

The result: "If they did work at all, they pulled revenue that might've just gotten in the next month or next quarter into this one. So it wasn't really a zero-sum game there. And we realized we're not really actually working on things that matter to customers that are going to move the needle over the long term."

The shift to input metrics reoriented the entire company. Instead of asking "how do we hit revenue this quarter," teams asked "how do we make selection broader, prices lower, and delivery faster?" These are things customers will always want: "There's no day that people will wake up 10 years, 20 years, 30 years from now and say, 'All else equal, I'd rather shop at a store with fewer items than more items or a store with higher prices than low prices or a store where things get to me more slowly versus more quickly.'"

## Output

After applying the Input/Output Metrics framework, you walk away with:

1. **A documented growth flywheel** identifying the reinforcing inputs that drive your business
2. **An instrumented customer journey** with metrics at each stage measuring speed, quality, and ease
3. **A goal-setting system** where 95%+ of goals are input metrics your team directly controls
4. **Weekly review rhythms** focused on input improvement rather than output panic
5. **A commitment to iterate on measurement** — accepting that your first metrics will be imperfect and refining them over time
