## When to Use

Use this framework when your team has goals but no rhythm for making progress on them. It is designed for companies that have tried OKRs and found them bureaucratic, or teams that set ambitious quarterly goals but lose sight of them by week three. The cadence is the framework — without it, OKRs are just a document nobody reads.

## The Framework

Christina Wodtke's Radical Focus model treats OKRs not as a planning artifact but as a weekly operating system. The atomic unit is not the objective or the key result — it is the weekly question: what am I doing this week to get closer to our goals?

### Step 1: Set One Objective per Quarter

Each team gets one objective per quarter — not five, not ten. The objective should be inspiring enough that people want to get out of bed for it, but specific enough to actually guide decisions.

> "Companies have a tendency to try to do everything all at this exact moment. And so, everybody's working with 1% on this, 1% on that and 1% on the other. And instead you use the OKRs and say, 'Okay, this is the big rock we're going to move. This is the big thing that's going to happen this quarter, and you can fiddle around with all the other stuff if you want, but this one has to move.'"

Common mistakes with objectives: making them so fluffy they have no meaning, or making them so boring they inspire no one.

> "Your objectives should make you, when your alarm goes off and you wake up, you go, 'Oh yeah, I'm changing the world today, or I'm doing something really cool.' It shouldn't be like, snooze."

### Step 2: Define Three Key Results

Triangulate success with roughly three key results. Wodtke recommends one hard number, one quality measure, and one tied to revenue.

> "I like three. I think about it as triangulating. I always like something that's really hardcore numbers. I like something that's a little squishier, like a quality, make sure you don't forget about it. And I usually like something that involves a dollar sign."

Answer the question "How do we know?" to derive key results from the objective. This question is the bridge between inspiration and measurement.

> "Objective is that vision for the quarter. This is what we're driving towards in this quarter. And then the key results, you answered the question, how do we know we succeeded?"

### Step 3: Monday Commitments

Every Monday, each person answers one question: what am I doing this week to get closer to our strategic goals?

> "Every Monday, because Monday is a great temporal landmark, look at your week, and you go, 'Okay, what am I going to do to move the ball forward?' And it could be an email to your boss, it could be an email to your accountability group, could be an email out to your team."

Wodtke recommends a status update format borrowed from Zynga: confidence level on key results, what you did last week, what you are doing next week, and a few notes. Limit to three P1 priorities — no more.

> "You can't have more than three P1s. You can have as many P2s as you want and P3s if you really think you should, but you can skim it really quickly."

The "last week / next week" structure reveals blockers. What stops you from executing reveals more about the organization than what you accomplished.

> "The whole, I tried to do this last week and I failed, learning goes through the roof."

### Step 4: Friday Celebrations

End each week by celebrating wins across the company. This is not optional — Wodtke considers it the most underrated element of the entire system.

> "People do not value celebrations enough. I've had CEOs who said, 'Well, it was the middle of the quarter, so we didn't start OKRs, but we did start Friday celebrations. And oh my God, things are already changing. Things are already getting better.'"

The celebration format is simple: "What was the most awesome thing that happened to you this week? What's the most awesome thing that happened in marketing? What's the most awesome thing that design did this week?"

### Step 5: Quarterly Grading and Retrospective

At the end of the quarter, grade your OKRs — but do not obsess over precision. A hand-wave of "about 70-80%" is fine. What matters is the retrospective: what worked, what did not, and what you learned.

> "Don't get fussy. Don't try to make it really precise. Just go ahead and say, 'Ah, hand wave, hand wave, 80%.' What matters is, why 80%? Really focus on the learning."

A good goal should feel ambitious but not doomed: "A good goal is one that makes you feel somewhat uncomfortable but not doomed. You're like, 'Woo, that's kind of good, that's going to be tricky. Okay, let's go for it.'"

### Step 6: Set Next Quarter (Fast)

Planning should take days, not weeks. Wodtke recommends grading OKRs in the second-to-last week of the quarter and setting new ones in the final week. Skip the elaborate approval process — have three peer teams review your OKRs with a 24-hour turnaround instead.

> "Instead of having your boss approve it, you write your OKRs, you get three teams that work with you enough to know what you're up to, to look them over and they just look them over, 24 hour turnaround. They say, 'This looks right, but I don't think this is possible.' They give you that feedback and you're done."

## Example

Wodtke describes piloting OKRs with your best team first, not your worst. A high-performing, multidisciplinary team will adapt the framework to fit the company's culture and surface what does not work — then serve as the template for rollout.

> "Because they're smart, they're capable, and if anybody's going to figure out how to make it work, they will. If you try to fix a bad team with OKRs, you'll make everybody hate OKRs and make the bad team worse."

She also shares a personal OKR example: running her life on OKRs for eight-plus years to manage ADHD, reviewing every Monday with an accountability group. One member of the group went from product manager to consultant to life coach — not by following a precise system, but by asking herself every Monday: "What am I doing this week to get to the outcome I really want?"

> "Her outcome was to not worry about money and be joyful with what she was doing. And she got that just by every Monday saying, 'What the fuck am I doing here? What am I trying to do again?' And it worked."

## Output

After adopting this cadence, you walk away with:

1. **One clear objective per quarter** that everyone can recall without looking it up
2. **Three key results** that triangulate success across quantity, quality, and revenue
3. **A weekly Monday commitment** — three P1 priorities aligned to the objective
4. **A weekly Friday celebration** that builds morale and cross-team awareness
5. **A lightweight status email** (confidence level, last week, next week) that makes progress visible
6. **A quarterly retrospective** focused on learning, not scoring precision
7. **A rolling learning cycle** where each quarter builds knowledge from the last

The cadence is what makes it stick. As Wodtke puts it: "That constant checking in is like touching a lucky stone in your pocket. It reminds you, 'Oh yeah, there's this thing, there's this thing.' And that rhythm... retrieval practice means that I put a fact in your brain and I keep asking you to go back and get it again."
