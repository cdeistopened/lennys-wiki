## When to Use

Use this playbook when you are building a consumer social app and need a systematic approach to finding product ideas, testing them, and engineering viral growth. It is most relevant for apps that depend on network effects — messaging, social, community, or any product where value increases with the number of connected users. The principles apply broadly, but the specific tactics are optimized for mobile-first consumer products targeting younger demographics.

## The Framework

Nikita Bier built or helped build more apps to the top of the App Store than almost anyone. He sold tbh to Facebook for over $30 million and Gas to Discord, both with tiny teams. But behind those headlines were 15 failed apps and years of hard-won pattern recognition. The playbook is a distillation of what he learned.

### Principle 1: Find Latent Demand

Do not invent a new behavior. Find people already trying to get a specific value through a distorted process, then crystallize what they actually want.

> "The way you should be searching for product ideas is this concept of latent demand where people are trying to obtain a particular value and going through a very distortive process to obtain that value. If you can actually crystallize what their motivation is and build a product around and clear up what they're trying to actually do, you can have this kind of intense adoption."

For tbh, the signal was extraordinary: an Arabic-language app called Sarahah hit number one in the US App Store. Teens were desperate for a way to receive anonymous feedback from friends, and they were willing to use an app they could not even read to get it.

> "The number one app in the United States was in Arabic. And that was one of the most strongest signal that you could ever have that people want something."

### Principle 2: Build for Teens (or Accept the Consequences)

Nikita found empirically that invitation rates drop roughly 20% per year of age from 13 to 18, and the flywheel becomes nearly impossible to spin for users over 22.

> "For every social app I've ever built, the number of invitations sent per user drops 20% for every additional year of age from 13 to 18. If you build for adults, expect to acquire every user with ads."

The reason is structural: teens see each other daily, their habits are malleable, and their social graphs are expanding, not contracting.

> "Once you exit college, you reduce the number of contacts, your daily contacts. Once you get married, it's even fewer... If you're building a product with network effects that's a communication tool, you want to be on that upward curve of adding connections to your social graph."

If you build for adults, you will likely need venture capital to finance paid acquisition, and you may never achieve true network effects.

### Principle 3: Develop a Reproducible Testing Process

The most important capability is not having the right idea — it is having a process for taking many shots quickly and reading signal cleanly.

> "The most important thing that I often instruct teams to do is to develop a reproducible testing process, and that will actually influence the probability of your success more than anything. It's so unpredictable whether a consumer product idea will work. If you actually focus more on your process for taking many shots at bat, that's what actually reduces the risk more than anything."

Nikita's team went from taking a year to build their first mobile app to building one in two weeks. They seeded apps into schools using targeted Instagram follows and micro-targeted ads to achieve density in a single community, then watched what happened.

> "For the first 100 users, yes, that's how we got them. And that allowed us to know whether the product was working or not... You never want to walk away from an experiment or test and say, 'Well, maybe the execution was bad.'"

### Principle 4: Validate in Stages

Do not try to prove everything at once. Treat product validation as a sequence of conditional statements, testing one layer at a time:

1. **Will people use the core flow?** — Make the core interaction perfect. Half-ass everything else.
2. **Will it spread within a peer group?** — Can it grow organically in one school or community?
3. **Will it hop peer groups?** — Can it jump from one school to the next without manual seeding?
4. **Will people pay?** — Can you monetize?

> "The way that now I approach a lot of consumer product development is if this is true, then what next needs to be true for this thing to work out? And these layers of conditional statements. And the more layers you have, the higher risk your product is, so you should try to condense it to about four things that must be true for the thing to work."

### Principle 5: Invert Time to Value

Users have approximately three seconds of attention. The aha moment must happen before they have time to leave.

> "In 2024, people's attention spans are like three seconds. It's really sad, but we are spread thin through so many notifications, products, everything that if you can't demonstrate value in the first three seconds, it's over."

For tbh and Gas, users received their first compliment-poll within seconds of opening. For Dupe, Nikita advised making the product work by simply prepending the domain to any product URL — the value was instant.

### Principle 6: Grow Ethically (The Internet Fights Back)

Nikita operates by a strict principle: never use user data in ways it should not be used, never send messages on behalf of users without explicit action, and always design growth systems that respect users.

> "I have this kind of crazy view that the internet is this living and breathing thing... if you do the wrong thing by users, the internet will come back and get even and defend itself. Whenever I design products, I try to do right by users because it'll always come back much worse."

This is not just ethics — it is strategy. Skeezy growth hacks create fragile systems that regulators, platforms, and users will eventually dismantle.

## Example

With tbh, one school sent 450,000 messages in the first seven days. Day-one message volume was 60 per user — typical messaging apps see 3-4. Growth was so intense that Nikita had to geofence the app to prevent server crashes, which was controversial internally but gave the team breathing room.

> "If it's working at this many individual schools, we could just relaunch it any time and it'll go viral. So let's regroup and figure out what's happening here and then relaunch."

With Gas (the spiritual successor to tbh), the same concept had to be rebuilt from scratch because the regulatory environment had changed — Twilio-based text invites were no longer viable. It took nine launches including multiple rebrands to get the growth flywheel working again. When they renamed from "Crush" to "Gas" with a black flame icon, invitation rates jumped because boys were now willing to invite their friends to the app.

> "Boys didn't want to invite their friends to an app called Crush with a pink icon."

At peak, tbh was getting 360,000 installs per day. Gas made $11 million in revenue, reached 10 million downloads, and was acquired by Discord — all with a tiny team running almost entirely on startup credits.

## Output

After applying this playbook, you walk away with:

1. **A latent demand signal** — evidence that people already want what you are building, obtained through distorted processes
2. **A target demographic** — with clear reasoning about where network effects can actually form
3. **A reproducible testing process** — a way to seed, measure, and iterate on consumer apps in weeks, not months
4. **A staged validation plan** — four conditional tests that must be true, checked in sequence
5. **A growth system** — designed to be ethical, platform-compliant, and durable enough to survive scrutiny
