# Fuel vs. Engine

## When to Use

Use this when you are hiring your first marketer, diagnosing why marketing is not working, or trying to figure out where to invest next in your go-to-market. Emily Kramer (first marketer at Asana, built marketing at Carta from scratch, co-founder of MKT1) created this framework because founders consistently ask "I think I need a marketer — who do I hire?" and the answer depends entirely on which side of marketing is the constraint. The framework replaces the confusing taxonomy of marketing sub-functions (product marketing, demand gen, growth marketing, content marketing) with a simpler diagnostic.

## The Framework

### The Two Halves of Marketing

**Fuel** is everything you create that adds value: the copy on your website, blog posts, templates, calculators, webinar recordings, podcast content, positioning statements, messaging frameworks, competitive comparisons. Fuel is the substance.

> "Fuel is like the copy on your website, it's the blog posts that you had, it's the templates that you've created. It's the video recordings of the webinars or the podcast. It's basically content, but also to include some of the copy." — Emily Kramer

Content is broader than blog posts. Tools, calculators, and interactive resources consistently outperform written content.

**Engine** is how you get fuel to the right people and track the results: distribution channels, email segmentation and automation, SEO technical infrastructure, paid ads, marketing ops (HubSpot setup, tracking, reporting), sales handoff processes. Engine is the delivery system.

> "An engine is how you get it out to the right people. And all of the tracking of that and sort of the ops work I put under engine, everyone needs an engine." — Emily Kramer

**Some things are both fuel and engine.** A Slack community creates content (fuel) and has a captive audience for distribution (engine). A newsletter produces valuable writing (fuel) and builds a subscriber list you can distribute to (engine). Recognize these dual-purpose assets.

### The Diagnostic

Ask two sets of questions:

**Fuel check:**
- What are your top-performing pieces of content?
- What is the top-performing page on your website?
- Can you articulate who your product is for, why it is better, and how it compares to competitors?

If you cannot answer these, you have a fuel problem.

**Engine check:**
- How are you distributing your best content?
- Do you know your funnel stages?
- How does the marketing-to-sales handoff work?

If the answer is "we shared it on social and it didn't go anywhere" or "we share a list and blast everyone," you have an engine problem.

> "You can just ask what are your top performing pieces of content... If you don't have an answer for those questions, you don't have top performing content and you don't know your positioning and all that stuff, you got a fuel problem here." — Emily Kramer

### Sequencing: Fuel Usually Comes First

You need something valuable to distribute before you build distribution. A common failure mode is building an engine (outbound SDR sequences, paid campaigns, email automation) with no fuel — nothing valuable to put in the emails or landing pages.

> "Sometimes what I see is people just build an engine first and they're like why isn't this working we're sending so many outbound emails? And it's like, well you don't have anything valuable to put in those, you have no fuel so this isn't working." — Emily Kramer

The reverse failure also happens: teams produce excellent content but never distribute it, then conclude "content doesn't work."

### Hiring Implications

Once you know whether you have a fuel problem or an engine problem, you know what kind of marketer to hire.

**Three marketing archetypes:**
1. **Content/Community** — Creates the fuel. Writers, content strategists, community builders. More fuel-side.
2. **Product Marketing** — Straddles fuel and engine. Understands the product, audience, and market. Owns positioning, messaging, launches. Leans fuel but has working knowledge of distribution.
3. **Growth/Demand Gen** — Builds and optimizes the engine. Email automation, paid acquisition, SEO, analytics, conversion optimization. More engine-side.

> "Forget the product marketing content partner, demand and growth, forget all of it, and just think of marketing as you need a fuel and you need an engine." — Emily Kramer

### Hire Pi-Shaped, Not T-Shaped

For your first marketing hire, look for someone who is expert in one archetype, proficient in a second, and has working knowledge of the third. Emily calls this a "pi-shaped" marketer (the pi symbol has two vertical lines, like a T with an extra leg).

> "You want the first marketer to be an expert in one of those three areas... And you want them to be proficient in another one, the second T. But you want them to be able to set strategy and know how to hire contractors across all of them." — Emily Kramer

One important constraint: content-plus-growth pi-shaped marketers are extremely rare because the skills draw from opposite sides of the brain. The realistic combinations are product marketing + growth, or product marketing + content.

### Business Model Determines the Mix

Top-down sales companies often already have SDRs (a rough engine). They typically need fuel first — positioning, messaging, content that makes outbound credible.

Bottom-up PLG companies often have organic discovery but terrible conversion funnels. They typically need engine first — lifecycle emails, onboarding optimization, funnel tracking.

> "If you're a product growth or bottom up, you might have a situation where just nothing is optimized. Your website's conversion rates are terrible, people are dropping out of the funnel all over the place. And you really need someone who can build up these lifecycle email touches." — Emily Kramer

## Example: Asana and Carta

At Asana, Emily was the first marketer at ~30 people. The company had strong organic word-of-mouth (the product spread through team collaboration), so the initial challenge was more engine: capturing and converting the interest that already existed, building lifecycle touches, optimizing the funnel from free to paid.

At Carta (~300 people, no marketing function), the challenge was different. The company had a sales-driven model but no fuel — no positioning framework, no content, no competitive messaging. Emily built the fuel layer from scratch before scaling distribution.

Both cases illustrate the diagnostic: same person, same skill set, but the starting point depended on whether the company's constraint was fuel or engine.

## Output

Running the Fuel vs. Engine diagnostic produces:
- A clear diagnosis of whether your marketing bottleneck is fuel (nothing valuable to say) or engine (no way to get it to people)
- A hiring spec for your first or next marketer based on the gap
- A prioritized list of fuel assets to create or engine infrastructure to build
- A shared language for founders and marketers to discuss marketing investment without drowning in sub-function jargon
