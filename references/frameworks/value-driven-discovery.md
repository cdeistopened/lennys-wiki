## When to Use

Use this framework when you are building a new product or feature and need to validate that what you are building actually solves a real problem. It is especially useful when your team is defaulting to assumptions about value — when the roadmap comes from the boss rather than from deep contact with people who have the problem. If you are running fake door tests, surveys, or opportunity solution trees and still launching products that flop, this method replaces all of that with one pressure-cooked approach.

## The Framework

The core insight is that value risk — will people actually buy, choose, or use this thing — is the most important and most overlooked risk in product development. Christian Idiodi, a partner at SVPG, frames the PM role around four risks: value, usability, feasibility, and viability. But most teams skip value entirely.

> "Value is probably, it is the most important and the most overlooked. And the big driver for that is often because of the operating model of teams. Teams are often given roadmaps of projects and features to go build and deliver. If that's the case, you actually really don't need a product manager because they're going to assume value."

### Step 1: Find People Who Have the Problem

Get out of your building. Do not brainstorm in a conference room. Go find real people who actually experience the pain you are trying to solve. The recruiting itself is a signal — if you cannot find enough people who have this problem, it might not be a problem worth solving.

> "If you truly, truly want to solve a problem, get out of your building, get out of the assumptions, get out of your opinions. Immerse yourself, find someone who has the problem, stick with them until you discover a solution for this problem."

### Step 2: Solve the Problem With Them, Not For Them

Work alongside these early customers. Do things that do not scale. Bring your designer and your engineer. Let them see the problem firsthand. Do not write requirements documents or user stories — try to solve the problem manually, learn from what breaks, and iterate in real time.

> "There's nothing better in learning how to solve a problem than trying to solve the problem. You will get all the answers, the research, the failure, the mistakes, all the evidence."

### Step 3: Reach the Reference Customer Threshold

Your target is a specific number of people who love your solution enough to put their reputation on the line by telling others about it. For B2B, that is 6 to 8 reference customers. For B2C, that is 15 to 25.

> "If we find a problem worth solving, we need to uncover a solution for B2B, I want six to eight references, for B2C, I want maybe 15 to 25 references as an indication that we've achieved product market fit."

The key constraint: all reference customers must want the same thing. If one person wants feature X and another wants feature Y, you do not build both. You find your single target market and converge.

> "All 25 have to want the same thing. If one person's out, you just don't do it at all."

### Step 4: Use the Reference Customers to Learn Everything Else

Your reference customers teach you how to market, how to sell, and how to price — not your marketing department. Put exactly what customers say on the box.

> "I only market exactly what customers tell me. I have never been surprised about what customers will say when I release a product because in this technique I already get their feedback."

### Step 5: Launch With Pre-Built Social Proof

Never launch an app without your references already in place. On launch day, your 25 five-star reviews are already there because you built the product with those people.

> "In every app I have ever put on the App Store, on the day I launch the app in the App Store, they are 25 5-star reviews... I will know I am not ready until I have achieved 25 people that have told me, worked with me and said, 'I am now ready to put my reputation on the line.'"

## Example

At SnagAJob, Christian received a call from Starbucks' head of global staffing about an urgent need to hire 800 employees for a newly acquired bakery. Rather than building software, he grabbed a designer and an engineer and started solving the problem manually — driving to construction sites, malls, and fast food restaurants to understand who else had the same need.

They discovered McDonald's needed 120 people on opening day for every new store, and Macy's hired 20,000 people every holiday season. They began manually recruiting candidates — posting on Craigslist, calling people, even reminding them to show up. Through trial and error across McDonald's, Starbucks, and Macy's (and learning to avoid airports), the team discovered what worked while the designer and engineer organically identified what to automate.

> "I never wrote a requirements document, I never wrote a story. The designer is thinking, how can we improve the end-to-end experience?... because they were involved from the very beginning in defining the problem, they were immersed in the solution to the problem."

After roughly nine months, the product launched and booked $32 million in sales in its first 90 days. McDonald's adopted it for every new store opening. Starbucks signed a global contract.

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **A validated problem** — confirmed by the difficulty or ease of recruiting people who have it
2. **A working solution** — built iteratively with real customers, not from assumptions
3. **Reference customers** — 6-8 (B2B) or 15-25 (B2C) people willing to publicly vouch for the product
4. **Natural marketing language** — sourced directly from what customers say, not what your team invents
5. **A clear product-market fit signal** — when you have your reference threshold, you are ready to launch
6. **A built-in pivot mechanism** — if you cannot find enough people with the problem, stop before you waste resources
