# Dunford Positioning Method (5 Components)

## When to Use

Use this when your team cannot explain what your product is in one sentence, when prospects keep asking "how are you different from X," or when your sales pitch feels like a product walkthrough with no story. April Dunford's positioning method -- from her book Obviously Awesome and refined across 25 years of repositioning B2B products -- treats positioning not as a marketing exercise but as a strategic decision that determines how you win in the market. It is the prerequisite to the Dunford Sales Pitch Framework.

## The Framework

Positioning has five interconnected components. Dunford works through them in a specific order because each one builds on the previous.

### Component 1: Competitive Alternatives

Start here. What would your best customers use if you did not exist? This is not just direct competitors -- it might be spreadsheets, hiring an intern, doing it manually, or using a tool from an adjacent category.

> "Most founders, when you talk to them, they didn't just come up with the idea out of nowhere. They woke up in the morning and said, 'You know what sucks about email, this thing sucks about email. And so I'm going to build this different kind of email that solves that problem.'" -- April Dunford

For Help Scout, the competitive alternatives were shared inboxes (like a team Gmail) and traditional help desk software (like Zendesk). Understanding these alternatives is the anchor for everything else.

### Component 2: Unique Attributes (Capabilities)

What do you have that the alternatives do not? These are features or capabilities -- concrete things your product does differently. Not value, not benefits. Just the raw capabilities.

For Help Scout: the ease-of-use of a shared inbox combined with help desk features, built from the ground up for digital businesses rather than retrofitted from a cost-center mindset.

### Component 3: Value (Differentiated Value)

What value do those unique attributes deliver to the customer? This is where you translate features into outcomes. The shift from "what it does" to "why it matters."

> "When we're talking about features, we shouldn't be talking about features. We should be talking about the value that the features deliver." -- April Dunford

Dunford groups value into "value themes" -- clusters of related capabilities that deliver a coherent benefit. For Help Scout, three value themes: (1) easy to use so reps adopt it, (2) full-featured so you never outgrow it, (3) built for great service so customers feel like humans, not ticket numbers.

The differentiated value often informs the market insight you lead your sales pitch with:

> "So the differentiated value often informs the insight that you start with?" -- Lenny. "Exactly. You're working backward from what your strengths are." -- April Dunford

### Component 4: Best-Fit Customers

Who cares the most about the value you deliver? This is not your total addressable market. It is the segment where your differentiated value matters intensely -- the customers who would be most disappointed if your product disappeared.

For Help Scout: digital-first businesses where customer service is a growth driver, not a cost center. These are companies without physical stores or sales reps, where every customer interaction is an opportunity to build loyalty.

### Component 5: Market Category

What market do you position yourself in? The category sets the frame of reference for buyers -- it tells them what you are and who you compete with. You can position in an existing category, a sub-segment of an existing category, or (rarely and only at scale) create a new category.

> "The job of a market category is to help answer the question, what is this thing all about? If I'm positioning in an existing category, I can say, 'Well, I'm CRM for very small businesses.' I didn't have to explain to you what a CRM was, that already existed." -- April Dunford

For Help Scout: customer service software for digital businesses. Not a new category -- a sub-segment of an existing one, differentiated by the type of business it serves.

### The Posture: Calm Confidence

Positioning should be delivered with what Dunford calls "calm confidence" -- not arrogance, not desperation.

> "The posture we should have is calm confidence. Where that calm confidence comes from is we really understand why people should pick us. We really understand who that's a really good fit for, and we don't even bother trying to chase anybody else." -- April Dunford

## Example

**Help Scout** repositioned using all five components:

| Component | Help Scout |
|-----------|-----------|
| Competitive alternatives | Shared inboxes (Gmail), help desk software (Zendesk) |
| Unique attributes | Inbox-like simplicity + full help desk features + built for service quality |
| Differentiated value | (1) Easy adoption, (2) Never outgrow it, (3) Customers feel like humans |
| Best-fit customers | Digital-first businesses where service drives growth |
| Market category | Customer service software for digital businesses |

**A sales enablement company** Dunford worked with provides another example. Their unique capability was tying sales enablement directly to revenue metrics -- something no competitor did. This informed their entire positioning:

> "When I go to do that in a sales pitch, I can translate that into my insight. My insight is every day your rep's not making quota it costs you money. So if we really want to solve sales enablement, don't we really want to have something where we can measure whether or not the sales enablement is working with sales metrics?" -- April Dunford

## Output

After applying this framework you walk away with:

- A list of competitive alternatives (what buyers actually compare you to)
- Your unique capabilities that alternatives lack
- 2-4 differentiated value themes that translate capabilities into outcomes
- A best-fit customer profile (who cares most about your value)
- A market category that frames what you are for buyers
- A positioning statement that can be tested directly in sales pitches

## How Positioning Connects to Sales

Positioning is not a document that lives on a shelf. It directly translates into the Dunford Sales Pitch:

- **Market insight** comes from your differentiated value (work backward from your strengths)
- **Alternative approaches** come from your competitive alternatives analysis
- **Perfect world** is a summary of your value themes
- **Product demo** is organized by differentiated value, not features
- **Best-fit customers** determine who you pitch to

> "Generally if the sales pitch isn't working, it's because the positioning was weak." -- April Dunford

The positioning can be tested in as little as two to four weeks by translating it into a sales pitch and running it with live prospects.
