## When to Use

Use this framework when you are defining or revisiting your activation metric, when your current activation rate seems suspiciously high (above 30-40%), when your growth team is debating workspace-level versus user-level activation, or when you need to operationalize an activation metric into something your team can actually move. Lauryn Isford developed this approach as Head of Growth at Airtable, where her team drove a 20% lift in activation rate over a six-to-eight month period through deliberate metric design and onboarding investment.

## The Framework

### Step 1: Set a High Bar (Which Means a Low Percentage)

This is Lauryn's counterintuitive core insight: your activation metric should be hard to reach. If 40% of users are "activated," the metric is not predictive enough.

> "An activation rate that falls in a lower percentage range, maybe for most companies five to 15%, is better than one that falls in a higher percentage range because it means that there's likely much higher correlation with long-term retention and you're really working hard to get most of your users to reach a state that they're not reaching today."

The logic: if your activation metric is too easy to hit, most users who reach it will still churn. A harder metric selects for users who will actually stick around:

> "If 40% of your customers are still active at week four maybe with a metric that would be week four active rate, then only a fraction of those are still going to be active in month 12, in month 18, and month 24, and I would much prefer to pick a more specific, more precise metric that maybe only 5% of users reach, but know that those 5% of users will be with us for the long haul."

The growth team's job then becomes moving that 5% to 6% or 7% — a seemingly small shift with massive downstream impact on the business.

### Step 2: Define What Activated Actually Means

At Airtable, the activation metric was **week-four multi-user active**: in the fourth week after signup, more than one person on a team is actively contributing to a workflow.

> "This metric is something that our wonderful analytics team determined to be highly correlated with long-term retention. It's also a metric that holds a relatively high bar, so it requires a number of things to be true, that something of substance has been built, that people persist and continue to collaborate, that they're collaborating on a weekly cadence."

For collaboration and seat-based products, workspace-level metrics often make more sense than individual user metrics. But Lauryn notes this depends on your product's growth mechanism and pricing model.

### Step 3: Decompose the Metric Into Its Component Parts

Before you can move an activation metric, you need to understand every input that feeds into it. Break it down exhaustively:

> "If you really think about what goes into that number, how many teams sign up as a team versus as an individual, how many of them make it to week four, how many of them have more than two people invited versus more than two people who've ever been active, versus more than two people who were active in the fourth week, maybe they have one person active in the fourth week, and then what does active mean, and what are the different kinds of behaviors that constitute contribution or being active."

This decomposition reveals which sub-metrics you can actually move. The activation rate is an output; the component parts are the inputs your team controls.

### Step 4: Add Supporting Metrics (Do Not Rely on One Number)

Airtable's growth team learned that a single activation metric does not capture the full picture. They added two supporting metrics:

**Individual retention**: Is this specific user retained at week two and week four?

**Build score**: A sophistication metric measuring whether users have reached intermediate levels of product usage.

> "Sometimes those treatments helped users become more sophisticated but didn't help them invite more teammates, or sometimes those new mechanisms made someone more interested in giving us a few more weeks, but what they built was actually not any more impressive than if they had done it on their own."

Lauryn's recommendation:

> "This group of metrics actually really set us free. It gave us those constraints where we could actually measure success in a few different ways and think about activation as being more complicated than just a single metric."

### Step 5: Be Willing to Change the Metric

Growth teams often over-invest in finding the "perfect" north star and then cling to it. Lauryn pushes back:

> "If you're working on the same metric forever, there's probably some inefficiency in actually chasing the biggest impact for the business... sometimes we overfocus on picking the perfect north star metric and by the time you feel like you've found the perfect one, it's actually time to move on to something different."

Airtable's growth org changed its organizational north star from revenue to user growth — a major shift that gave the team more strategic clarity:

> "We decided that it was most essential in our work to grow the business to bring millions more people onto the platform, using the product and finding value in the product, because we were taking a decade's long view that they could always become monetized or converted customers later."

She recommends stability for at least six months, then reassessing:

> "Having stability in north star metric for at least six months feels like table stakes to me... for building momentum, for building domain expertise, and also for compounding impact in areas where you see success."

## Example

At Airtable, the team inherited a week-four multi-user active metric from the analytics team. They decomposed it into its components and discovered specific leverage points — how many accounts started as teams versus individuals, how many actually had multiple people active in week four versus just invited, and what "active" meant in terms of contribution quality.

They then invested six to eight months in three major onboarding initiatives: a guided onboarding wizard (the biggest impact), ongoing education mechanics, and personalization by learning style rather than job title. Together, these drove a 20% lift in activation rate.

> "We were working on activation for a few years before I arrived at Airtable. There was already an awesome activation team, hard at work. Overall, the investments we made in onboarding over about a six to eight month period that included the wizard, the mole with ongoing education, and also some personalization together drove a 20% lift in activation rate for Airtable."

The guided wizard was the most impactful element. Instead of tooltips, it walked users through step-by-step setup where they selected project type and tracked items while watching a workflow visualize in real time:

> "You can see your workflow visually come to light as you do the work on the left half of the screen, and all of this is done for you in a very guided and instructive way."

A critical learning: segmenting users by **building style and learning style** was more effective than segmenting by job function:

> "Knowing how someone would go about building was more effective than what do you actually want to see on the other side."

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **A high-bar activation metric** — a specific, measurable milestone that only 5-15% of users currently reach, validated as highly correlated with long-term retention
2. **A full metric decomposition** — every component input that feeds into the activation rate, with current values and identified leverage points
3. **A supporting metrics suite** — two to three additional metrics (individual retention, sophistication score, or similar) that capture dimensions the primary metric misses
4. **A personalization strategy** — user segments based on building/learning style rather than demographics, with different onboarding paths for each
5. **A reassessment timeline** — a plan to evaluate whether the metric is still the right one after six months of focused work
