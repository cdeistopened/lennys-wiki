## When to Use

Use this when you're making a major product strategy decision: entering a new market, choosing a product direction, evaluating build-vs-buy, or deciding whether to pivot. Also use when a competitor launches something similar and you need to decide whether to follow, differentiate, or ignore.

Balaji's original framing: "A good founder doesn't just have an idea. They have a mental map of the idea maze. They know the history, the dead ends, the reasons previous attempts failed, and the specific reason why now is different."

Most product teams skip this. They see an opportunity, build toward it, and discover six months later that three companies tried the same thing and failed for structural reasons the team never investigated.

## The Framework: 5-Step Idea Maze for Product Decisions

### Step 1: Map the History

Before committing to a product direction, answer:

- Who has tried this before? (Both direct competitors and analogous attempts in adjacent markets)
- What happened to each attempt? (Acquired, failed, pivoted, still alive but struggling)
- Why did the failures fail? (Timing, technology, distribution, business model, team)

This is not competitive analysis. Competitive analysis asks "who are we competing with now?" The idea maze asks "who has ever walked this path, and what did they find?"

Lenny's Podcast is rich with these histories. Casey Winters describes how Pinterest's growth strategy was informed by understanding why previous visual bookmarking tools (like Delicious) plateaued. Elena Verna explains how Miro's PLG strategy was designed after studying why previous whiteboard tools couldn't convert free users to paid.

### Step 2: Identify the Dead Ends

For each failed attempt, classify the failure:

| Failure Type | Description | Example |
|-------------|-------------|---------|
| **Timing** | The market wasn't ready | WebTV (1996) tried streaming before broadband |
| **Technology** | The tech didn't exist yet | Mobile-first tools before smartphones |
| **Distribution** | Product was good, couldn't reach users | Many developer tools with no community |
| **Business model** | Product worked, couldn't monetize | Consumer social apps pre-advertising |
| **Execution** | Right idea, wrong team or wrong speed | Google+ had the resources but not the network density |

Dead ends are not failures you need to avoid. They are walls you need to verify still exist. If the wall was "bandwidth was too expensive" and bandwidth dropped 10x since then, that dead end is now open.

### Step 3: Find the "Why Now"

Balaji's key insight: the best ideas look like dead ends to everyone else. The founder's edge is knowing which dead end just opened up.

"Why now" categories from Lenny's archive:

- **Technology shift**: AI makes something possible that was manual before (Ramanujam on AI pricing models)
- **Behavior shift**: Remote work changed how teams collaborate (Figma's timing)
- **Platform shift**: Mobile, API economy, no-code tools create new distribution paths
- **Regulatory shift**: New compliance requirements create demand (GDPR created a privacy tools market)
- **Cost shift**: Infrastructure costs dropped enough to serve a new market segment

### Step 4: Draw the Remaining Paths

After eliminating dead ends, draw the paths that remain open. For each path:

1. What is the entry point? (Which user segment, which use case, which wedge)
2. What does the first product look like? (MVP scope)
3. What is the natural expansion path? (Second product, adjacent segment, platform play)
4. What moat forms along this path? (Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers: which power do you accumulate?)
5. Who else is on this path right now? (Current competitors walking the same corridor)

This connects to the Bowling Pin Strategy: your first pin is your entry point into the maze. The question is which corridor you walk down after knocking it over.

### Step 5: Commit to a Corridor

The idea maze is not an excuse for analysis paralysis. The output is a committed direction with eyes open.

Document:
- **Chosen path**: Which corridor and why
- **Known risks**: Which walls might still be solid (and how you'll test)
- **Abandon criteria**: What evidence would make you turn back (pre-mortem)
- **Speed advantage**: Why you can move faster than others on this path

## Output

After applying this framework:
- A written history of previous attempts in your space (at least 5 predecessors mapped)
- Dead ends classified by failure type with assessment of whether each wall still holds
- A "why now" thesis with specific evidence
- 2-3 remaining paths drawn with entry points, expansion paths, and moat potential
- A committed corridor with abandon criteria
