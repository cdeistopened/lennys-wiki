## When to Use

Use this framework when you have a product-led growth motion generating self-serve users and you want to layer on a sales motion to capture enterprise-level contracts. It answers the question: how do I go from individual users paying $0-50/month to enterprise accounts paying $15K-100K+/year without abandoning the product-led model that got you here? It applies to any B2B SaaS company that has free or low-cost individual usage and wants to move upmarket.

## The Framework

Elena Verna describes product-led sales (PLS) as a bridge between product-led growth and traditional sales. The product acquires and activates users. Sales converts that usage into enterprise contracts. The critical shift: product creates the pipeline for sales, not marketing.

> "In the product-led sales, product acquires and activates a customer and product creates pipeline for sales, so the relationship is not that there's a go-to-market org with marketing and sales and product just kind of throws features across the fence for them to sell."

> "Self-serve monetization is very much a prosumer use case where an individual is trying to solve the problem on their own. Product-led sales converts the usage that you've generated via self-serve into a sales opportunity and it attaches a salesperson to close a much larger contract."

### Step 1: Understand the Ceiling

Product-led growth has a natural revenue ceiling. Individual users will only spend so much on their own. The enterprise buyer has different needs — compliance, security, admin controls, team management — that a self-serve flow cannot address.

> "Sales can bridge that gap, and then you can increase the perceived value in order to bridge the gap to that $15,000, 20, hundred-thousand dollar contract."

As Lenny summarized: "Product-led growth has a ceiling. You're only going to get so high in terms of spend."

Elena's response: "It's a bridge."

### Step 2: Define PQAs (Product-Qualified Accounts)

The core concept of PLS is the Product-Qualified Account — an account where usage signals indicate readiness for a sales conversation. This is different from a PQL (Product-Qualified Lead) or MQL (Marketing-Qualified Lead).

A PQA is defined by account-level usage patterns that correlate with sales readiness. Start with intuition, then validate with data:

> "First of all, it starts with just top-down intuition. What are the signals that you're seeing around the accounts that are highly correlative to sales being very excited?"

Key signals to track: number of active users in an account, depth of usage, features accessed, team-level activity. The goal is a threshold that, when crossed, triggers sales engagement.

> "You usually actually look at it almost from conversion rate. How many teams do I have in my ICP that are active? That's my denominator, and how many of those teams are reaching PQA target this month? It's like a conversion rate to PQA out of engaged ICP teams."

### Step 3: Make Product Accountable for Pipeline

This is the most counterintuitive and most critical step. Product — not marketing — must own pipeline generation in a PLS motion.

> "The worst thing that you can do is to say, 'I'm going to do product-led sales and I'm going to do it in marketing.' Recipe for disaster. You'll be failure mode within six months because product has to take accountability over selling of the product itself."

Product's job is to get more accounts to reach the PQA threshold. Sales' job is to convert PQAs into contracts. If product is not accountable for PQA volume, the motion breaks.

### Step 4: Time the Sales Touch Correctly

Accounts move in and out of PQA status. The sales interaction must add value to the user journey, not create friction.

> "Sales interaction has to add value to user journey, not be disruptive or create additional friction because I've seen so many people posting of like, 'Why am I getting a phone call 10 minutes later?' It's annoying."

> "All it is is the right time to engage with the customer and then go hit them hard over all of the channels of communication, but then also know that accounts go in and out of PQA. Today I might be in the PQA, next week I might not be, so you need to sunset the efforts if you didn't get in touch with the customer and wait for the next PQA moment."

### Step 5: Start with What You Have

You do not need new hires or new tools to start. Use existing sales reps, existing data tools, and manual processes.

> "I don't know if I would start there right from the beginning unless you actually understand the data because buying a platform will not bridge the gap for you understanding what actually matters in your data."

Start with Google Sheets, Looker dashboards, and Amplitude charts. Prototype outreach manually. Scale after you find what Verna calls "data-sales fit."

### Step 6: Profile Your Users at Onboarding

Ask four universal questions during signup that every user is accustomed to answering:

> "You should always be able to ask about company size, about the department, which customer's coming from, about their seniority on the team and about their use case. Those four questions are uniform. Everybody's used to answering them."

This data lets you distinguish individual users from enterprise buyers and route accounts appropriately.

## Example

Verna describes the pattern she has seen at companies like Miro, Amplitude, Dropbox, and SurveyMonkey. At Miro, individual users would sign up for free, create boards, invite colleagues. When an account had multiple active users across teams, that was a PQA signal — a sales rep would reach out not to pitch the product (the users already loved it) but to explain enterprise value: admin controls, SSO, compliance, team management.

The organic demand from individual usage eventually plateaus:

> "End user fundamentally does not equal enterprise buyer and that organic demand ends up plateauing very shortly because you cannot just make enterprise buyers happen from your users."

The PLS bridge solves this by having sales tell the enterprise story that individual users cannot tell themselves.

Verna also shared benchmarks: freemium conversion rates hover around 5%, trial conversions are 10-15%, but contract values increase dramatically with PLS.

> "Conversion rates stay the same, but your contract values and your lifetime value of the customer is what starts to increasingly go up and that you can influx into creating a smile revenue curve."

## Output

After applying this framework, you should have:

1. **PQA definition** — the specific usage signals that indicate an account is ready for sales outreach
2. **A conversion rate target** — PQA rate out of engaged ICP teams, measured monthly
3. **Product accountability** — product team OKRs that include PQA volume, not just feature delivery
4. **A sales timing protocol** — when to engage, when to sunset outreach, and how to add value rather than friction
5. **Onboarding profiling** — four questions asked at signup to classify users by company size, department, seniority, and use case
6. **An MVP stack** — manual data workflows before investing in PLS-specific tooling
