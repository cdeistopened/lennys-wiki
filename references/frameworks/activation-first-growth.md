# Activation-First Growth Sequence

## When to Use

Use this when you have validated product-market fit (40%+ on the Sean Ellis test or strong retention cohorts) and need to decide where to invest to grow. Most teams default to acquisition — "let's drive more traffic" — and defer onboarding and activation improvements. Sean Ellis, who coined the term "growth hacking" and was head of growth at Dropbox and Eventbrite, argues this is exactly backwards. The sequence matters because customer acquisition is so competitive that if you are not efficient at converting, retaining, and monetizing, you cannot find scalable profitable channels.

## The Framework

### Prerequisite: Confirm You Have Something People Need

Before executing any growth sequence, confirm that a meaningful percentage of users consider the product a must-have. Sean uses his PMF survey as a leading indicator, then validates with retention cohorts.

> "Until you deeply understand that product market fit, you kind of don't have the tools to be able to grow the business." — Sean Ellis

### Step 1: Define Your North Star Metric

Choose a metric that captures units of value being delivered — the number of people experiencing the product-market fit moment.

> "When I think about a north star metric, that's what I'm thinking about is something that reflects how many people are coming in and experiencing that product-market fit experience, whatever that is." — Sean Ellis

### Step 2: Diagram the Current Engine

Map the entire system as it exists today: onboarding flow, aha moment, engagement loop, referral mechanics, revenue model, acquisition channels. Sean calls this the "value delivery engine." You cannot improve what you have not made visible.

### Step 3: Start with Activation

Activation is the highest-leverage point because new users are at maximum risk of being lost, and the work sits in the gap between product (focused on the roadmap) and marketing (focused on acquisition).

> "Generally the sequence that I like to do is start with activation because that one's just so critical and it's easy to get lost in between, especially for an early product. The product team's so focused on the roadmap... And then a marketing team so focused on bringing new people in, but how do you get those new people to a great first experience falls through the cracks a lot of times." — Sean Ellis

Activation work has two levers: increase desire (remind people of the benefit they are about to get) and reduce friction (remove steps, simplify, clarify).

The most effective diagnostic is qualitative: ask people who dropped off why they stopped. At LogMeIn, 95% of signups never did a remote control session. When the team emailed non-downloaders asking what happened, they discovered users thought the free product was too good to be true. Adding a choice between "download free version" or "try paid trial" made the free option credible and produced a 300% improvement in the download step.

> "A problem well stated is a problem half solved." — Sean Ellis, quoting Charles Kettering

### Step 4: Build the Engagement Loop

Once activation is working, focus on the mechanics that bring users back — notifications, triggers, content, social connections — so they experience the core value repeatedly.

### Step 5: Enable Referral

If the product naturally crosses from one user to another (file sharing, collaboration, social features), build referral programs to accelerate what is already happening organically. Do not try to manufacture referral for a product people do not already talk about.

> "I think it's a great accelerant when it's already working, but it can't fix it if people don't want to talk about your product." — Sean Ellis

### Step 6: Optimize the Revenue Model

Make sure pricing and packaging are not blocking conversion or creating unnecessary churn before you pour money into the top of the funnel.

### Step 7: Scale Acquisition Channels

Only now obsess on scalable customer acquisition. By this point, you are efficient enough at converting, retaining, and monetizing that channels that previously could not work profitably will start to work.

> "Customer acquisition is so hard that if you're not really efficient at converting and retaining and monetizing people, you're going to really struggle on the customer acquisition side." — Sean Ellis

## Example: LogMeIn

LogMeIn offered free remote access software. Sean could not spend more than $10,000 per month profitably on customer acquisition. The data showed why: 95% of signups never completed a remote control session.

The CEO froze the entire product roadmap and pulled every engineer, designer, and marketer onto one problem: improve the signup-to-usage rate. In three months, they improved it from 5% to 50% — a 1,000% increase.

Sean then went back and tried the exact same acquisition channels that had previously capped at $10,000/month. They now scaled to $1 million per month with a three-month payback on marketing dollars. Word of mouth accounted for 80% of new users.

> "There was this major inflection point by just focusing on activation." — Sean Ellis

The same principle applied at Dropbox: before investing in the famous referral program, the team had already built a product with extremely high organic word-of-mouth. The incentivized referral program accelerated existing behavior rather than creating it.

## Output

Working through the sequence produces:
- A north star metric tied to value delivery
- A current-state diagram of your growth engine
- An activation metric and a focused effort to collapse time-to-value
- Engagement mechanics that drive repeat usage
- Referral or word-of-mouth amplification (if organic sharing exists)
- A revenue model that does not block conversion
- Acquisition channels that are now profitable because the engine is efficient
