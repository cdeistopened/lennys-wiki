# Dunford Sales Pitch Framework

## When to Use

Use this when your B2B sales team is running product demos that end in "we'll get back to you" or no decision. April Dunford built this framework after observing that most SaaS sales pitches are glorified product walkthroughs -- feature, feature, feature -- with no positioning at all. The framework is designed to combat the fact that 40-60% of B2B purchase processes end in no decision, not because the status quo won, but because the buyer could not figure out how to choose confidently.

## The Framework

The pitch has two major halves: **The Setup** (about the market) and **The Follow Through** (about your differentiated value). The setup is shorter but cannot be skipped.

### Part 1: The Setup

The setup is not about you. It is about the market, your point of view on it, and getting the buyer aligned with your worldview before you ever mention your product.

**Step 1: Market Insight**

Open with your point of view on the market -- the problem inside the problem. This is the reason you built what you built.

> "It starts with our insight into the market. So you can think of this as our point of view on the market or kind of like the problem inside the problem, in some ways. So in Help Scout's case, the insight is, 'Hey, customer service is just different for digital businesses, and this is really a growth driver, not a cost center. So we need to think about customer service in a different way.' That's their insight." -- April Dunford

The insight should not be controversial for your target customer. If a prospect fundamentally disagrees with your insight, they are likely a poor fit. Dunford notes that for Help Scout, if a prospect says "No, actually it's a cost center for us. We're just trying to get the cost down," they should disqualify the deal because Zendesk will serve that customer better.

**Step 2: Alternative Approaches (Pros and Cons)**

Paint a picture of the entire market landscape. Walk through what is good and bad about other ways of solving the problem. This is not competitive bashing -- it is helping a confused buyer understand their options.

> "What B2B software buyers want in a sales interaction is perspectives on the market and help weighing their options. And we don't do that. We're just like, 'Here's our stuff. You figure it out, it's up to you.'" -- April Dunford

For Help Scout, this means explaining that shared inboxes are easy but you outgrow them, while help desk software has the features but was built around cost reduction, not great service.

**Step 3: Perfect World**

Conclude the setup by getting agreement on what a perfect solution would look like. This step is where you win the deal before you have even mentioned your product.

> "Can we agree that if we really wanted to solve this problem and knowing what works and doesn't work in the alternate solutions, can we agree really good solutions should tick these boxes? This is getting the customer aligned with your worldview." -- April Dunford

> "If they're right with you on this definition of the perfect world, then I've already won the deal and I haven't even talked about us yet because the way I've set it up, I'm the only one that can deliver that." -- April Dunford

### Part 2: The Follow Through

**Step 4: Product Introduction + Differentiated Value**

Introduce your product within the market category, then walk through value you deliver -- not features. Each value claim maps to a capability and back to the perfect world criteria you just established.

> "We shouldn't be talking about features. We should be talking about the value that the features deliver." -- April Dunford

For Help Scout: "One, as easy to use as an inbox -- look, here's the inbox. Two, features so you never have to outgrow it -- here's how we do prioritization and assignments. Three, deliver an amazing experience -- the customer gets to stay Dave and not ticket number 1479."

**Step 5: Proof**

Demonstrate you can do what you claim. Customer case studies, third-party validation, independent benchmarks.

**Step 6: Handle Silent Objections**

Address concerns that have not come up during the call. These are typically operational: "Sounds good, but it's probably hard to adopt." Or: "Sounds good, but it's probably expensive." Or: "What about security?"

**Step 7: The Ask**

Close with the specific next step in your sales process. Could be asking for the sale, setting up a proof of concept, or identifying who else needs to be involved.

> "Whatever the next step is in your sales process. That's how we end -- with the ask." -- April Dunford

### Critical Insight: Discovery Is Built In

Unlike typical pitch decks, this structure embeds discovery directly into the setup. The setup should be a conversation, not a monologue.

> "That setup part should not be me talking at you. It should be a conversation between me and the client saying, 'Do you agree with this? Is this what you're seeing? Did you start with a shared inbox? What are you doing right now?'" -- April Dunford

## Example

**Help Scout** repositioned from a generic customer service tool to customer service software built for digital businesses. Their old pitch: log in, click through every dropdown menu, show every feature. Their new pitch:

1. **Insight**: Customer service for digital businesses is a growth driver, not a cost center.
2. **Alternatives**: Shared inboxes are easy but you outgrow them. Help desk software has features but pushes customers to low-cost channels and assigns ticket numbers instead of treating people like people.
3. **Perfect world**: "Can we agree we want something as easy to use as a shared inbox, with all the help desk features so we never have to migrate, built from the ground up to deliver amazing customer service?"
4. **Product + Value**: Demo organized around those three value pillars.
5. **Proof**: Customer case studies.
6. **Objections**: Address adoption, cost, security concerns.
7. **Ask**: "Who do we need to talk to about a proof of concept?"

The result: prospects stopped asking "How are you different from Zendesk?" because the pitch itself answered that question before it could be asked.

## Output

After applying this framework you walk away with:

- A market insight statement that frames why your product exists
- A map of alternative approaches with honest pros and cons
- A "perfect world" definition that only your product can satisfy
- A value-organized demo structure (not feature-organized)
- A list of silent objections to pre-empt
- A specific ask for the next step

## Anti-Patterns

- **Leading with FOMO when buyers are indecisive.** Dunford cites Matt Dixon's research (The JOLT Effect, 2.5M sales calls studied via Gong): if a customer is already indecisive, FOMO makes it worse. They are already stressed; more pressure pushes them toward no decision.
- **Skipping the setup.** Without market context, your demo is just "here's our stuff, you figure it out." That is exactly how 40-60% of deals end in no decision.
- **Feature-first demos.** Buyers cannot connect features to value without the positioning context the setup provides.
