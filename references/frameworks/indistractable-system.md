## When to Use

Use the Indistractable System when you know what you need to do but keep not doing it. You sit down to write the spec, build the presentation, or do the strategic thinking your role demands, and 90 minutes later you have achieved inbox zero and caught up on Slack but touched nothing that moves your career forward. As Nir Eyal puts it: "Distraction was tricking me into prioritizing the urgent and the easy stuff at the expense of the hard and important work I had to do to move my life and career forward." This system is for product managers, founders, and anyone who recognizes that their inability to focus is the bottleneck, not their lack of ideas.

## The Framework

The Indistractable System is built on a single insight: the opposite of distraction is not focus — it is traction. "Both words come from the same Latin root, trahare, which means to pull, and they both end in the same six letters, A-C-T-I-O-N that spells action, reminding us that distraction is not something that happens to us. It is an action that we ourselves take." What separates traction from distraction is one word: intent. Anything done on your schedule, with purpose, is traction. Everything else — including work-related busywork — is distraction.

The system has four steps, applied in order.

### Step 1: Master Internal Triggers

This is the most important step. Ninety percent of distractions begin from within — not from external pings. "The problem is not our technology. The problem is our inability to deal with discomfort." When you sit down to do deep work and feel the pull toward email, it is not the email calling you. It is boredom, anxiety, uncertainty, or the cold-start feeling of hard cognitive work.

The primary technique here is the **10-Minute Rule**. When you feel the urge to do something other than what you planned, set a timer for 10 minutes. Your job during those 10 minutes is either to get back to the task at hand or to "surf the urge" — acknowledge the emotion and let it crest and subside. Eyal uses a personal mantra: "This is what it feels like to get better. This is what it feels like to get better."

The psychology behind it: "You're not saying no, you're saying not yet." Telling yourself "don't do that" backfires. Postponement works because "emotions are like waves. They crest and then they subside. But that's not how we think about emotions. Most of us think about emotions as always being there." Nine times out of ten, by the time the timer goes off, you will have forgotten about the distraction and be back at work.

Over time, the 10-minute rule becomes the 12-minute rule, then the 20-minute rule. The most important effect is proving to yourself that you have agency.

### Step 2: Make Time for Traction

"You cannot call something a distraction unless you know what it distracted you from." This step replaces to-do lists with a time-boxed calendar. Eyal argues to-do lists are actively harmful: "To-do lists have no constraints. You can always add more." The result is a daily sense of failure and a reinforced self-image as someone who cannot manage their time.

Instead, turn your values into time. Once a week (Eyal does Sunday evenings, 10 minutes), review your calendar for the week ahead and ask: "Does this schedule reflect my values?" Fill three domains:

1. **You** — Sleep, exercise, reading, prayer, whatever matters to you personally
2. **Relationships** — Scheduled time with partner, children, friends. "Most friendships don't die in some big blowout. Relationships starve to death because we don't invest in them."
3. **Work** — Split into reactive work (email, Slack, meetings) and reflective work (planning, strategizing, deep thinking). Book reflective work first.

The metric shifts from "Did I finish the task?" to "Did I do what I said I was going to do for as long as I said I would without distraction?" This creates a feedback loop. You learn how long things actually take, eliminating the planning fallacy where "tasks take people three times longer to finish than they estimate."

### Step 3: Hack Back External Triggers

External triggers account for only 10 percent of distractions, but they still matter. "To hack something is to gain unauthorized access." Key tactics:

- **Device-level**: Set do-not-disturb on your desktop permanently during work hours. Eyal runs do-not-disturb from midnight to 11:59 — all day.
- **People-level**: Signal when you are indistractable. The book includes a card you place on your monitor: "I'm indistractable at the moment. Please come back later." For families, Eyal's wife uses a "concentration crown" — a small light-up wreath from Amazon. When she wears it, the family knows not to interrupt for 30 minutes. "Unless you're bleeding."
- **Meeting-level**: Audit meetings that did not need to happen. Audit emails that did not need to be sent.

### Step 4: Prevent Distraction with Pacts

Pacts are the last line of defense. They must come after the first three steps or they will not work. There are three types:

- **Price pact**: A financial disincentive for going off track. Eyal used one to finish his book and to get in shape. "Let's say that if you don't work out, 8:00 A.M. Monday morning, you're going to have to pay me $10,000. Are you going to do it? 'Well, of course.' Okay, so we've established you can. Now we're just negotiating the price."
- **Identity pact**: Adopt "indistractable" as part of your identity, the same way a vegetarian does not debate whether to have bacon for breakfast. "A vegetarian doesn't wake up in the morning and say, 'Hmm, I wonder if I should have a bacon sandwich for breakfast.' No, they are vegetarian. That is who they are."
- **Effort pact**: Add friction to getting distracted. Eyal uses a $10 outlet timer that shuts off his home internet router at 10 PM every night. He also recommends the app Forest (a virtual tree dies if you pick up your phone) and Focusmate (a video co-working service that creates social accountability for focus sessions).

## Example

Lenny Rachitsky walks through his own daily struggle during the episode. Every morning, he sits down to write his newsletter and immediately checks Twitter and email instead. Eyal diagnoses it live: the internal trigger is the cold-start discomfort of deep cognitive work. The fix: (1) use the 10-minute rule to surf that initial urge, (2) put "write newsletter for 30-45 minutes" on the calendar as a specific time block rather than a vague intention, (3) turn on do-not-disturb and close distracting tabs, (4) optionally use Focusmate to bind himself to a focus session at 8 AM. Lenny confirms that he previously used large "deep work time" blocks at Airbnb — two- to three-hour blocks where he would "slap you if you book anything over this meeting" — and that those worked precisely because they were calendared and defended.

## Output

After applying the Indistractable System, you walk away with:

1. **A set of internal trigger tools** — the 10-minute rule, urge surfing, a personal mantra — ready for when discomfort hits
2. **A time-boxed weekly calendar** reflecting your actual values across self, relationships, and work
3. **A distraction-proofed environment** — do-not-disturb settings, visual signals to colleagues and family, audited notifications
4. **Pre-commitment pacts** — price, identity, or effort pacts as a last-resort firewall
5. **A new success metric**: not "Did I finish?" but "Did I do what I said I was going to do for as long as I said I would without distraction?"

As Eyal summarizes: "The antidote for impulsiveness is forethought. If we plan ahead, if we know that we're going to take steps a day to prevent getting distracted tomorrow, there's no distraction we can't overcome."
