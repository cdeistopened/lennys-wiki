# Andy Raskin Strategic Narrative

## When to Use

Use this when your pitch feels like every other competitor's pitch ("we solve this problem better"), when prospects keep asking "how are you different from X," or when you want a single story that aligns sales, marketing, product roadmap, fundraising, and recruiting under one narrative. Andy Raskin developed this framework by studying screenwriting structure and applying it to business pitches. His blog post dissecting Zuora's sales deck -- "The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen" -- got 2 million views and became his calling card. The framework replaces what Raskin calls the "arrogant doctor" pitch (problem, solution, why we are better) with a movement-based narrative.

## The Framework

### The Arrogant Doctor (What to Stop Doing)

The default pitch structure most people learn:

1. You have a problem (a pain)
2. I have a solution (a treatment)
3. Here is why my solution is better than the other solutions

> "The way I learned how to pitch in business school, and I think the way most people did is what I call the arrogant doctor. So you have a problem, a pain, I have a solution, a treatment, and I'm going to tell you why it's better than all the other treatments." -- Andy Raskin

The problem: this structure sets you up for feature comparison bragging. You end up in a "we're better than" arms race.

### The Strategic Narrative (5 Steps)

**Step 1: Name the Shift (Old Game to New Game)**

Open with a fundamental change happening in the world. Name the old game and the new game in concise, almost oversimplified terms.

> "Every movie starts with some kind of shift in the world. And I call this shift the shift from the old game to a new game." -- Andy Raskin

The naming must be compact. Transactions to subscriptions. Software to cloud. Opinions to reality. Top-down learning to upskill-from-within.

> "This very, very concise naming is really key. And it's hard because in making it compact you're losing completeness." -- Andy Raskin

You are slightly overstating it, and that is fine. Nobody says "subscription economy, really? I still go to the grocery store and buy things." The power is in the simplicity.

**Step 2: Name the Stakes (Winners and Losers)**

Show that winners are already playing the new game. Make the future feel split between a very positive outcome and a very negative one -- not just "sort of okay."

> "The second piece is what I call naming the stakes. One way that's really great if we can do it is to name the winners to show that winners are already playing this new game." -- Andy Raskin

Raskin uses the Star Wars analogy: Luke does not want to join the mission until his aunt and uncle are killed. Then the stakes become life and death. For your buyer, you need to show that staying in the old game is not safe.

> "Literally, what is the definition of making it emotional? This is for me the definition -- the prospect doesn't see the future as sort of okay. They see it as split between a very negative outcome and a potentially very positive outcome." -- Andy Raskin

With Zuora: "Companies are dying. The ones that are winning are doing this subscription thing. Here are the logos."

**Step 3: Name the Object of the New Game**

Define the specific goal or rallying cry of the movement. Often best framed as a question.

> "I find it often is really nice to do it as a question. So we say, there's this shift from transactions to subscriptions and look, everyone's doing it. So we ask a simple question: what would it take to turn every customer into a subscriber?" -- Andy Raskin

This becomes the company's mission. For Zuora: "Turn customers into subscribers." For Airbnb: "Belong anywhere" / "Live there." The object should feel "asymptotically unachievable" -- a direction to pursue, not a box to check.

**Step 4: Show the Obstacles**

Winning the new game must be hard, or there is no story. Spell out the challenges that prevent achieving the object. These are the same problems you would normally pitch as "the problems we solve" -- but repackaged as obstacles to an emotionally charged goal.

> "These sound like problems. This is what people would normally say, 'These are the problems we solve.' But by setting up this story first we've repackaged them as obstacles to a new goal state that we've already positioned as life and death. So they take on this much more emotional meaning." -- Andy Raskin

For Zuora: How do you measure lifetime value in a subscription world? How do you track changing customer preferences? How do you manage billing for always-on relationships?

**Step 5: Show How You Overcome the Obstacles (Magic Gifts)**

Now -- and only now -- talk about your product. Present your capabilities as the tools that overcome each obstacle. Include customer success stories.

> "Narrative people, in the movie business they call these the magic gifts that the main character gets to help them win." -- Andy Raskin

Your product is not the hero. Your product is the lightsaber. The buyer is Luke.

## Example

**360Learning** ($200M+ raised, corporate training software) worked with Raskin to reframe their pitch:

**Before**: "We're collaborative learning. Here's why our learning platform is different."
Result: Prospects kept asking "how are you different from this other learning platform?"

**After** (the strategic narrative):

1. **Shift**: From top-down learning (one guru creates all training content and pushes it out) to upskill-from-within (your own people become the educators)
2. **Stakes**: Google already turns internal experts into public champions. McDonald's recruits on the promise of learning from your team. Companies that cannot get skills to their people fast enough will die.
3. **Object**: "What would it take to turn your experts into champions of learning?"
4. **Obstacles**: How do you make it possible for anyone to create a course? How does the learning department keep control? How do you measure impact?
5. **Magic gifts**: 360Learning's collaborative platform features, mapped to each obstacle.

**Result**: CEO Nick Hernandez reported that after adopting this narrative, prospects stopped asking "how are you different from this other learning platform?" The conversation shifted from feature comparison to movement enrollment.

**Gong** used a similar structure: the shift from opinions to reality in sales management. This reframed a conversation recording tool into the leader of a movement around data-driven sales. CEO Amit Bendov told Raskin it became their product roadmap filter: "Does it help us move from opinions to reality? It's in. Does it not? Less prioritized."

**Drift** pitched the shift from "the world of forms" (fill out a form, wait days for a response) to "the world of now" (real-time conversation). This turned the 30th chatbot on the market into the leader of a movement called conversational marketing.

## Output

After applying this framework you walk away with:

- A named shift (old game to new game) in 2-4 words per side
- A stakes narrative showing winners already in the new game and the cost of staying in the old
- A rallying cry / object of the new game (often phrased as a question)
- 3-5 obstacles that prevent achieving the object
- Your product capabilities mapped to each obstacle
- A narrative that works across sales decks, website copy, fundraising, recruiting, and product roadmap prioritization

## Where It Differs from Dunford

April Dunford's framework starts with an insight about the market and walks through alternative approaches. Raskin's starts with a paradigm shift and positions your company as the leader of a movement. Dunford herself notes the distinction:

> "Andy Raskin has a different framework, feels similar, but I know they're different. His framework starts with a shift that's happened in the world, and then here's who's going to win in the shift, here's who's going to lose." -- Lenny Rachitsky

Both work. Dunford's tends to be stronger for competitive markets where the buyer is comparing specific solutions. Raskin's tends to be stronger when you want to create a movement that makes the competition irrelevant.
