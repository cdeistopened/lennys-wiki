## When to Use

Use this framework when you have an MVP or early product and need a fast, quantitative signal on whether you have product-market fit before investing in growth. It is especially useful when your retention cohorts are too immature to read clearly, or when you need to diagnose why growth is stalling. The test also works as a recurring health check at any stage — Sean has used it on everything from seed-stage startups to billion-dollar companies.

## The Framework

### Step 1: Ask the Core Question

Survey users who have actually experienced the product (not just seen a demo) with one question:

> "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?"

Give them three choices: Very Disappointed, Somewhat Disappointed, Not Disappointed.

As Sean explains the origin: "I was trying to have just a filter so that I was not treating all feedback from customers the same, but I was trying to find feedback from customers who actually really cared about the product."

### Step 2: Check Your Score Against the 40% Threshold

Calculate the percentage of respondents who answered "Very Disappointed." The benchmark:

- **40%+ Very Disappointed** — You have a leading indicator of product-market fit. You have created something people consider a must-have.
- **Below 40%** — You likely do not yet have PMF. Focus on finding it before investing in growth.

Sean describes how the threshold emerged empirically: "Over time, I started to see there was a pattern that once you got a high enough percentage of users saying they'd be very disappointed, most of those products did pretty well. If you felt too low, those products tended to suffer."

He is clear this is a leading indicator, not a final verdict: "I would say it's a leading indicator of product market fit. The lightning indicator is, do they actually keep using it? So probably retention cohorts are more accurate, but the problem is, like your time at Airbnb, how long do you have to look at a retention cohort before you know that you've actually long-term retained someone?"

### Step 3: Dig Into the "Very Disappointed" Segment

Regardless of your score, the real value is understanding who your must-have users are and what they value. Sean recommends a follow-up survey:

> "One of my favorite questions is, 'What is the primary benefit that you get?' And then I use that initially as an open-ended question to kind of crowdsource different benefits people are getting. But then I run another survey where I turn it into a multiple choice question, force them to pick one of four distinctive benefit statements."

Then go one layer deeper: ask "Why is that benefit important to you?" This surfaces the emotional context that powers your messaging.

### Step 4: Reposition and Streamline Onboarding

Once you know who your must-have users are and what they care about, do two things:

1. **Set the right expectations** — Reposition your marketing and landing pages around the benefit your must-have users value most.
2. **Speed to value** — Streamline onboarding so the first thing a user experiences is the core benefit.

Sean is emphatic about this: "Moving retention is really hard, but it's usually much more function of onboarding to the right user experience than it is about the tactical things that people try to do to improve retention."

### Step 5: Ignore the "Somewhat Disappointed" Users (Carefully)

This is counterintuitive but critical. Sean warns against letting nice-to-have users dilute your product for must-have users:

> "Just ignore the people who say they'd be somewhat disappointed. They're telling you it's a nice to have. If you start paying attention to what your somewhat disappointed users are telling you and then you start tweaking onboarding and product based on their feedback, maybe you're going to dilute it for your must have users."

### Step 6: Resurvey After Changes

The test is repeatable. After repositioning and onboarding changes, survey the next cohort of users to see if your score moves. Changes can happen fast — in Sean's Lookout case, the score went from 7% to 40% in two weeks without changing the core product.

## Example

Sean describes working with Lookout, a mobile security company, where the initial survey returned only 7% Very Disappointed:

> "I dug into the 7% who said they'd be very disappointed without the product and found that most of that 7% were focused on the antivirus functionality."

The team made two changes: they repositioned the product around antivirus (setting the right expectations) and streamlined onboarding so the first action was setting up antivirus protection with a confirmation message "you're now protected."

> "The next cohort of people that we surveyed were at 40% saying they'd be very disappointed without the product. So that literally took two weeks to make those changes. Six months later, it was 60% on the score. And then I think they hit the billion dollar valuation four or five years later on ultimately being one of the early unicorns."

At Xobni (inbox spelled backwards), the follow-up question "Why is that benefit important to you?" revealed users were "drowning in email" — which became the hook for acquisition messaging that powered growth.

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **A PMF score** — a single number that tells you whether to focus on growth or keep iterating on product
2. **A must-have user profile** — who specifically considers your product essential, and what benefit they value most
3. **An emotional context** — the "why" behind the benefit, which becomes your positioning and ad copy
4. **A repositioning and onboarding plan** — concrete changes to set the right expectations and speed to value
5. **A repeatable measurement** — resurvey each cohort to track whether PMF is strengthening or weakening over time
