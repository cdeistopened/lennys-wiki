## When to Use

Use this framework when you have an early product with some users who love it and some who do not, and you need a systematic way to increase product-market fit over time. It is especially powerful when you are unsure what to build next — the engine produces a prioritized roadmap that is mathematically guaranteed to increase your PMF score. It works best for products past the first MVP stage, where you have enough users (roughly 40-100 survey respondents) to segment meaningfully.

## The Framework

Rahul Vohra built this engine at Superhuman and documented it in what he describes as "still the most widely shared post on First Round Review." The core insight is that product-market fit is not binary — it is a number you can measure, track, and systematically increase.

### Step 1: Measure Your PMF Score

Survey users with the Sean Ellis question: "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?" with three options: Very Disappointed, Somewhat Disappointed, Not Disappointed.

> "What Sean Ellis found is that the companies that struggled to grow almost always had less than 40%, very disappointed. Whereas the companies that grew the fastest almost always had more than 40%, very disappointed. And this question, this metric is way more predictive of success than something, for example, like net promoter score."

### Step 2: Identify Your Three Segments

Your respondents now fall into three groups:

- **Very Disappointed** — These people already love your product. Do not over-optimize for their feedback. They are your base.
- **Somewhat Disappointed** — These are the people the engine targets. They kind of love your product, but something is holding them back.
- **Not Disappointed** — Politely disregard them. They are too far from loving your product to be worth converting.

Rahul is direct about the counterintuitive discipline required:

> "To get to product market fit, you have to deliberately not act on the feedback of many of your early users. This is at the same time as listening to people intensely and building what people want. That's what we're here to do, is to make something that people want. But it can't be all people."

### Step 3: Find Your Main Benefit

Go back to the Very Disappointed users and ask them why they love your product. What is the core benefit?

> "In the early days of Superhuman, it would have been speed and keyboard shortcuts and the overall design aesthetic as well as the time that we were saving you."

### Step 4: Subdivide the Somewhat Disappointed

This is the key move. Take your Somewhat Disappointed users and split them into two camps:

1. **Those for whom the main benefit resonates** — they like Superhuman for its speed, but something else is holding them back.
2. **Those for whom it does not** — they like Superhuman for a different reason entirely.

> "I would simply ask, 'Wait, do you like Superhuman because of its speed or for something else?' And if it's something else, well, and this is hard to do, but politely disregard those people and their feedback. Because even if you built everything that they asked for, they're still pulling you in a different direction."

### Step 5: Build the Roadmap

You now have one focused subsegment: Somewhat Disappointed users for whom the main benefit resonates. Ask them what they do not like about the product. Their answers become your roadmap.

> "You have then articulated the subsegment of the subsegment that it makes sense to pay attention to. There's another question in the engine to figure out what they don't like about the product. Now you have a list of things people love, you have a list of things people don't love, and you can work down that list to make the product market fit score go up."

### Step 6: Split Time 50/50

At the start of every planning cycle, allocate your efforts:

- **50% doubling down** on what the Very Disappointed users love
- **50% overcoming objections** from the Somewhat Disappointed users (the ones for whom the main benefit resonates)

> "At the start of every planning cycle, I advise spending half your time doubling down on what people really love and half your time systematically overcoming the objections of the somewhat disappointed users, but specifically those for whom the main benefit resonates."

### Step 7: Resurvey and Repeat

Run the survey again after each cycle. Your PMF score should climb. If it does not, revisit your segmentation.

## Example

At Superhuman, the engine produced a concrete roadmap. The Very Disappointed users loved speed, keyboard shortcuts, and design. The target Somewhat Disappointed users also valued speed but were held back by missing features like calendar integration, mobile experience, or specific email workflows.

Rahul describes how the engine scales: "We don't run the engine as is for Superhuman as a whole. There are enough subcomponents of Superhuman now that are almost individual products. For example, Superhuman for Sales, our multiplayer and collaboration features, how we think about the enterprise, AI is its own thing, but we do sometimes run it on those individual pieces."

Superhuman also paired the engine with concierge onboarding — one-to-one sessions for every new user — to ensure people actually experienced the core benefit:

> "In those early days we insisted on one-to-one concierge onboarding, and it was absolutely the right thing to do. You couldn't use Superhuman unless you went through the onboarding experience."

At peak, they had about 20 people doing manual onboarding. They only transitioned to self-serve when the top of funnel became wide enough that manual onboarding no longer made sense economically.

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **A PMF score** — your current percentage of Very Disappointed users, tracked over time
2. **A clear user segmentation** — who loves you, who almost loves you, and who to ignore
3. **A prioritized roadmap** — built from the specific objections of reachable users, not guesswork
4. **A planning discipline** — the 50/50 split between strengthening your core and converting fence-sitters
5. **A repeatable engine** — run it every planning cycle to continuously increase PMF

Rahul frames the core ideas simply: "Number one, you can measure product market fit. Number two, you can optimize product market fit. Number three, you can systematically, even numerically increase product market fit. And number four, you can even have an algorithm write your roadmap for you, and that is a roadmap that is guaranteed to increase product market fit."
