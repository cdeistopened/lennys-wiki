## When to Use

Use this framework when your feedback conversations keep circling the same symptoms without producing lasting change. Use it when you are writing a performance review and realize your notes are all about specific incidents rather than underlying patterns. Use it when a direct report keeps making similar mistakes in different contexts and tactical corrections are not sticking. The Exponential Feedback Method replaces surface-level, one-time feedback with root-cause identification that produces compounding improvement over time.

Ravi Mehta developed this approach through the Reforge product leadership program and his coaching work at Outpace.

## The Framework

### The Problem with Normal Feedback

Most feedback — whether in a casual one-on-one or a formal performance review — is surface-level. It focuses on symptoms rather than causes:

> "One of the most challenging things I think for both a PM as well as a product leader is to figure out how to grow yourself and grow your team. And a key way to do that is through feedback. It's really important to provide people with good feedback to help them understand how to grow. But the problem is, and this is true in a casual one-on-one, as much as it's true in an annual performance review, it's oftentimes the feedback that people provide is very surface level. It may focus on particular symptoms but not root causes."

When you give someone feedback on a specific moment — "that meeting didn't go well" or "that spec was missing key details" — they fix that one thing and the feedback is consumed. There is no compounding return.

### Step 1: Map Against Competencies

Use a structured competency framework (Ravi's PM competency model has 12 competencies across four areas: product execution, customer insight, product strategy, and influencing people) as a diagnostic lens. Rate yourself or the person you are evaluating as "needs focus," "on track," or "outperforming" across each competency:

> "I'll often encourage people when they're first starting to use the framework, just to go through each competency and rate themselves needs focus, on track or outperforming on each of the competencies to quickly get a read on where you feel like you're landing."

This takes five to ten minutes and immediately surfaces patterns that symptom-level feedback misses.

### Step 2: Cross-Reference with Your Manager

Have your manager complete the same rating independently. The diagnostic power comes from the comparison:

> "You can ask your manager to do that same thing. You can do that in five or 10 minutes. And then the areas where your manager and you see eye to eye and the areas where you guys see differently is stimulus for a really deep conversation."

Areas of agreement confirm shared understanding. Areas of disagreement — where you rate yourself "on track" but your manager rates you "needs focus" — are where the most valuable conversations happen.

### Step 3: Identify Root-Cause Behaviors

Use the competency gaps to trace surface symptoms back to underlying behaviors. This is where feedback becomes exponential:

> "I think about exponential feedback as feedback that has compounding returns. So if you give someone feedback on a particular symptom or you give them feedback on something that's tactical and they fix that in a moment, the feedback, the conclusion of that feedback, it just happens and then it's gone. But if instead you help a person understand the underlying behaviors that led to that particular situation, then they can focus on growing themselves."

### Step 4: Build Self-Diagnosis Capability

The ultimate goal is not just to fix one issue but to give the person a framework for ongoing self-improvement:

> "They can also focus on helping to diagnose their own performance more effectively, and that leads to compounding returns where they just keep getting better and better over time."

### If Your Manager Is Not Doing This

If your manager is not providing this kind of structured feedback, Ravi offers two tactics:

First, ask them to do the competency rating exercise: "Your manager will almost certainly have some impression of your performance that they haven't necessarily... If they're not doing it proactively, they probably have it intuitively. And helping them get it down on paper and getting it more specific can be a really good way to start that conversation."

Second, give explicit permission for real-time feedback: "Just inviting your manager to say, 'Look, I'm really looking to level up. Please give me feedback whenever you see something. You can give it to me in real time. Don't worry about wordsmithing it. I just want to make sure that I'm getting better.'"

And when you receive feedback, reward the giver: "It's so key because then you've rewarded the person for giving you feedback, even if it hurts inside, and then they'll want to do it in the future."

## Example

At Tripadvisor, Ravi built the PM rotational program specifically to accelerate competency development. New PMs out of business school and undergrad went through four six-month rotations across zero-to-one teams, growth teams, and infrastructure teams. The competency framework gave both the PMs and their managers a shared language for identifying gaps and tracking progress across rotations — turning what would have been generic "you're doing great" feedback into specific, actionable development plans that compounded across the two-year program.

The downloadable evaluation kit at ravi-mehta.com includes self-assessment tools that map competencies to PM archetypes — growth PMs tend to cluster around data and outcome ownership, while product discovery PMs cluster around different competencies. Understanding your archetype helps you identify which gaps are worth closing and which reflect a deliberate specialization.

## Output

After applying the Exponential Feedback Method, you walk away with:

1. **A competency heat map** — a visual snapshot of where you or your report stands across all 12 PM competencies
2. **Alignment gaps** — specific areas where self-assessment and manager assessment diverge, prioritized for conversation
3. **Root-cause growth areas** — underlying behaviors to develop, not just symptoms to fix
4. **A self-diagnosis capability** — the person can now repeat this process independently, creating ongoing compounding improvement
5. **A feedback culture trigger** — the framework gives both parties shared language and psychological safety to have harder conversations
