## When to Use

Use this framework when you are about to launch a significant product, feature, or initiative and want to surface risks the team is not talking about. It works best early in a project's lifecycle — before you have committed to a plan that is hard to reverse. It is especially valuable in organizations where people default to optimism and avoid raising concerns for fear of seeming negative.

## The Framework

A pre-mortem inverts the typical planning question. Instead of asking "what could go wrong?" you start from a hypothetical failure and work backwards.

### Step 1: Set the Prompt

Open the meeting with a specific failure scenario. As Shreyas describes it:

> "The initial prompt is the genius, which is the prompt starts with, imagine this project that we are working on has failed six months from now, or this launch we are doing has miserably failed. Let's just all imagine that. Now, let's work backwards from there and ask ourselves what went wrong, what could have contributed to this utter failure."

This framing is what makes it work. It gives everyone psychological permission to be negative:

> "A pre-mortem setting gives everybody the license to actually think about what can go wrong. So, that psychological safety is a big, big factor in why a pre-mortem works."

### Step 2: Invite Every Function

For a large launch, Shreyas recommends splitting into two sessions:

1. **Product/Engineering pre-mortem** — every engineer on the project, plus PM and design leads. Focus on technical risks, infrastructure, edge cases.
2. **Go-to-market pre-mortem** — sales, support, marketing, design, and core engineering leads. Focus on launch risks, customer experience, communications.

For a smaller launch, one combined meeting is sufficient.

### Step 3: Classify Risks into Three Categories

Each team member contributes risks in three buckets:

- **Tiger** — A real threat that will actually kill the project. As Shreyas puts it: "A tiger is a threat that will actually kill us, just like a tiger would. So these are actual problematic things that could be really harmful to the product or the project."

- **Paper Tiger** — A seeming threat that others worry about, but you believe is not actually dangerous. "This is a seeming threat that others might be worried about, but you're not worried about."

- **Elephant** — The thing in the room nobody is talking about. It may not kill the project, but the team is not seeing reality clearly. "An elephant could be like, well, we are assuming that just because we launch this and do a bunch of PR that we'll get users, but are we sure we're going to get users?"

### Step 4: Silent Writing, Then Sharing

Alternate between quiet time and discussion:

> "I'll share the prompt and then I'll say, okay, now the next five minutes or the next 10 minutes is quiet time where I already have that template... where people start entering their own tigers and paper tigers, and elephants in a way that nobody else sees. And so, people do that, and then we go around the room and share."

### Step 5: Vote on the Scariest Tiger

After sharing, have each person pick the most frightening tiger that someone else raised — not their own. This creates a natural prioritization through voting.

> "I ask people to pick the tiger that they find more scary, but that somebody else mentioned, so not their own tiger, but some other tiger that somebody else mentioned that they found more scary."

### Step 6: Create the Action Plan

The pre-mortem leader takes the output, prioritizes the top risks, and creates a concrete action plan:

> "As the pre-mortem leader, it's my responsibility to take all of that output that the team has generated in this document and then prioritize. Because again, the point is not to solve every problem, the point is to identify threats that we are not talking about openly or that we might just be missing."

## Example

Shreyas describes a pattern he has seen repeatedly across the industry — companies that launch features, face massive backlash, then backtrack. A pre-mortem would have caught this:

> "Sometimes you'll see a company announce something and they have massive backlash. And then one reasonable observer might say, 'Well, how did this company miss this?'... oftentimes I agree, it should be obvious to these teams what issues these things are going to cause. In fact, it is obvious to some team members, but the problem is that they perhaps haven't created that psychological safety and that vocabulary to be able to talk about it in an objective way."

At Stripe, Shreyas ran pre-mortems regularly with his teams. The shared vocabulary became self-reinforcing:

> "What I noticed as I ran pre-mortems is that in future meetings that the team had where I wasn't even present, people started talking about, 'Oh, I have this tiger. Can I bring up this tiger?' And all of a sudden it became okay for people to bring those things up, which I think is perhaps the best part about a pre-mortem is that shared vocabulary."

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **A prioritized list of real risks** (tigers) with owners and mitigation actions
2. **A list of paper tigers** — fears the team can now explicitly set aside, reducing anxiety
3. **Named elephants** — assumptions the team was making without acknowledging them
4. **A shared vocabulary** that persists beyond the meeting, making it safe to raise concerns anytime
5. **A concrete action plan** owned by the pre-mortem leader

Shreyas frames the ROI simply: "Pre-mortem is a very inexpensive way to see these things because all it is is one meeting followed by some work that the leader needs to do to prioritize, followed by some mitigating actions, which you would've had to take anyway. So that's why I'm a huge fan of pre-mortems — it's one of those very low downside, but very high upside things."
