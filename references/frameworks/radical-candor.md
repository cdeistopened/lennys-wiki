## When to Use

Use this when you need to give feedback (praise or criticism) to a direct report, peer, or manager and want it to actually land. It is especially useful when you notice yourself holding back because you do not want to hurt someone's feelings, which Kim Scott calls ruinous empathy and identifies as "by far and away the most common problem." The framework also helps you solicit feedback from others so that candor flows in both directions.

## The Framework

Radical Candor has three interlocking layers: the 2x2 diagnosis, the HHIIPPP delivery checklist, and the CORE structure for what you actually say.

### Layer 1: The 2x2 — Diagnose Where You Are

The vertical axis is **Care Personally**. The horizontal axis is **Challenge Directly**. The four quadrants:

| | High Challenge | Low Challenge |
|---|---|---|
| **High Care** | **Radical Candor** (the goal) | **Ruinous Empathy** (most common failure) |
| **Low Care** | **Obnoxious Aggression** | **Manipulative Insincerity** (worst place) |

Kim Scott explains: "Radical Candor is just what happens when you care personally and challenge directly at the same time." She warns that the most frequent mistake is ruinous empathy: "90% of us make 90% of our mistakes in that ruinous empathy bucket."

She also notes the dangerous slide between quadrants: "When I realize I've acted like a jerk, when I realize I've landed in obnoxious aggression, it's not actually my instinct to go the right way on care personally. Instead, it's my instinct to go the wrong way on challenge directly and to pretend that I agree when I disagree, and then I wind up in the worst place of all, manipulative insincerity."

The key framing: "I want people to use this framework not to judge themselves or other people harshly, but like a compass to guide specific conversations with specific people to a better place."

### Layer 2: HHIIPPP — How to Deliver It

When giving praise or criticism, run through this checklist:

1. **H — Humble**: "I call it candor and not truth because if I march into a room and I say, 'Lenny, I'm going to tell you the truth,' I'm kind of implying I've got a pipeline to God and you don't know anything. Omniscience is not a requirement for radical candor."
2. **H — Helpful**: "You want to state your intention to be helpful... You're telling them this because you care about them."
3. **I — Immediate**: "If I'm telling myself I'm going to wait for a better moment to tell this person this thing, what I'm really saying is I'm never going to say."
4. **I — In person** (or synchronous): "Slack is just a feedback train wreck waiting to happen. I once coached someone who kept giving feedback over Slack and I finally just quit coaching him." She recommends phone over video, citing University of Chicago research showing "more noise than signal in facial expressions and body language."
5. **P — Praise in public**
6. **P — Criticize in private**
7. **P — Not about personality**: Focus on behavior, not identity.

### Layer 3: CORE — What to Actually Say

Structure the feedback itself as:

- **C — Context**: Where did this happen? ("In the meeting...")
- **O — Observation**: What specifically did you see? ("...when you said um every third word...")
- **R — Result**: What was the impact? ("...it made you sound stupid.")
- **E — nExt step**: What should change? ("Go to the speech coach.")

Kim Scott notes this works for praise too: "In the meeting [context], when you offered both sides of the argument [observation], it earned you credibility [result]. Do more of that [next step]."

### Layer 4: Soliciting Feedback — The Order of Operations

The first step is always soliciting criticism, not giving it.

"If you say, 'Do you have any feedback for me?' You're wasting your breath. The other person's going to say, 'Oh no, everything's fine.'"

Instead, ask a specific go-to question. Kim Scott's default: "What could I do or stop doing that would make it easier to work with me?" But she insists: "Do not write down my question because if you sound like Kim Scott and not like yourself, then other people are not going to believe you want the answer."

Other versions she mentions:
- "Tell me why I'm wrong." (Christa Quarles, CEO of OpenTable)
- "What's one way I could help you be more successful?"
- "What could I have done this week to better support you in your work?" (Jason Rosoff, Radical Candor co-founder)

Budget five minutes at the end of every one-on-one for this.

## Example

Kim Scott tells the story of "Bob," a charming, funny employee who brought levity to the office but consistently produced sloppy work. For ten months, Scott responded with ruinous empathy: "Oh Bob, this is such a great start. You're so awesome. We all love working with you. Maybe you can make it just a little bit better."

The result: "Eventually the inevitable happened. I realized that if I didn't fire Bob, I was going to lose all my best performers." When she finally had the conversation, Bob asked: "Why didn't you tell me?... I thought you all cared about me."

Reflecting on what she would do differently now, Scott says she would have addressed it at the very first sign: "I would've said, 'Bob, maybe I'm misinterpreting the expression on your face, but it looks to me like you are not happy with this. What's going on?'" And if that did not work: "I would have not been shy about pointing out very specifically every problem that I saw in that piece of work, every single one."

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:
- A diagnosis of which quadrant you tend to fall into (almost certainly ruinous empathy)
- A go-to question for soliciting feedback, written in your own voice, scheduled into your one-on-ones
- A delivery habit (HHIIPPP) so you stop saving feedback for performance reviews
- A structure (CORE) for the actual words, applied equally to praise and criticism
