## When to Use

Use this framework when your company is considering adding a product-led growth motion to an existing sales-led product, when you have launched a free tier or trial but are not seeing the conversion or adoption you expected, or when you need to audit your PLG readiness before committing engineering resources. Hila Qu developed this checklist through advisory work with dozens of B2B SaaS companies and her own experience building PLG at GitLab. If you skip any of these five foundations, your PLG motion will underperform or fail entirely.

## The Framework

Hila is blunt about the most common failure mode: companies treat PLG as a feature launch rather than a motion that requires structural foundations.

> "They think about PLG equals launch of free version or launch of free trial. They made this assumption in their head, 'Oh, I already have a product... Now if I add a free version, if I open a free trial, that's it.' Like that's basically PLG, and I will have conversions."

The five foundations are prerequisites, not nice-to-haves.

### Foundation 1: A Free Vehicle

You need something — free plan, free trial, open source version, or at minimum an interactive demo — that lets a potential customer experience your product without talking to sales and without a contract.

> "The first thing is that have a... you are able to have a vehicle, have a free version, have a free trial. Sometimes it's a open source product. A lot of developer products start as a open source product."

If none of those options work for your product, Hila points to Amplitude's approach as a floor:

> "They build a really realistic interactive demo that's getting closer to PLG. So it's not PLG, but it's getting closer and you can already see the value and play with it yourself."

### Foundation 2: Fast Time to Value

Having a free vehicle is useless if users sign up and immediately get lost. You need to engineer a quick path to value — the user needs to experience a "mini aha moment" before they give up.

> "Just because you have this vehicle doesn't mean people will come and use it and see the value. So you need to figure out how do you give your users a warm start and help them get started."

Hila's specific recommendation: give users sample data, templates, or pre-built examples so they can see the product working before they have to do the hard work themselves.

> "What about we gave them some sample video or sample action they can try. It's not the same as they do it themselves, but it's better. It's getting closer."

### Foundation 3: Self-Serve Checkout

When a user hits the aha moment and wants to pay, they must be able to do so without involving your sales team. This sounds obvious but Hila finds it broken constantly:

> "Many company I work with, when I try to buy, I cannot even find where to buy. It's very hard to find where do I click to start this checkout process."

She also flags localization as a silent killer:

> "One company, when I look at their data, they find that India has very low success rate, which is expected, but we begin to say it's because the payment solution they choose actually doesn't support that market well. And they added another payment solution, immediately they are seeing much better success rate."

### Foundation 4: A Data Foundation

This is the foundation most companies skip, and it is arguably the most important. Hila calls PLG fundamentally "DLG — data led growth":

> "PLG I always say is actually fundamentally DLG, data led growth. So when you give away your free product, what you want to get in exchange are two things. One is a broader reach because free product spread itself is lower barrier to entry. Two, you want to understand the usage behavior of those free users, which features do they use and which features kind of correlates with a higher conversion rate, retention rate, all of that."

Without usage data, you cannot identify your aha moment, cannot build PQLs (product qualified leads), and cannot design the upgrade path:

> "If you don't have a foundation of data and understanding of how to analyze those data, you are giving a way of free product for nothing."

### Foundation 5: Simple Pricing

If your pricing requires a sales call to explain, a custom quote, or complex enterprise negotiation just to get started, the self-serve funnel breaks:

> "If your pricing is super complicated, they need to... Whenever they pick, for example, they try the product, they love it, they have a self-checkout flow, but in order to decide how much they need to pay, they need to send your sales some information, you need to do a quote, then that's broken."

This does not mean you cannot have enterprise pricing tiers. It means the entry-level paid plan needs to be understandable and purchasable without human assistance.

## Example

At GitLab, all five foundations were in place but at different stages of maturity. The free vehicle was the open source product — engineers could use GitLab for personal projects without any approval. Time to value was reasonable because developers already knew what a DevOps platform should do. Self-serve checkout existed for small teams.

The journey Hila described: an individual developer uses GitLab free for side projects. When their employer evaluates consolidating DevOps tools, this developer raises their hand. The team signs up for a 30-day free trial, runs a proof of concept, and if the team is small enough, they buy through self-serve checkout. If the company is large enough, the data foundation surfaces them as a PQL — product qualified lead — and the sales team reaches out with context about what the team has already built and which features they use.

> "This person knows how to use it. This person already set up the foundation with the free version and they started the free trial. They use their 30 day to do a proof of concept."

Lenny summarized the five foundations: "There needs to be something free and kind of self-serve that you can just start using on your own. There needs to be a quick time to value, there needs to be a self-serve checkout experience. You need a data foundation... And then the last piece is pricing that's simple, that people understand."

## Output

After auditing against the five foundations, you walk away with:

1. **A gap assessment** — which of the five foundations are missing or weak in your current product
2. **A sequenced buildout plan** — what to build first (typically: free vehicle, then time-to-value work, then checkout, then data instrumentation)
3. **A commitment check** — honest assessment of whether your organization will invest for 12-24 months, because PLG does not pay off in one quarter
4. **A team structure recommendation** — who you need (Hila notes that assigning one person to coordinate across sales, marketing, and product is setting them up to fail: "That person need to be a magician in order to be successful")
5. **A PLG funnel map** — whiteboarded end-to-end journey from website visit to free signup to aha moment to self-serve checkout or PQL handoff to sales
