## When to Use

Use this framework when you are building a consumer product and need to diagnose why growth is stalling, why retention is not flattening, or why you are spending too much on acquisition. It is designed for consumer founders and product leaders who are drowning in vanity metrics — sign-ups, downloads, MAUs — and need a lens on what actually matters for building something that endures. Sarah Tavel developed it at Greylock (now Benchmark) after transitioning from leading discovery product at Pinterest, where she saw firsthand how easy it is to optimize for the wrong metric.

## The Framework

Sarah's core observation: founders were coming to her with beautiful up-and-to-the-right graphs of sign-ups and MAUs, but those metrics did not tell her whether the company was on a path to building an enduring product.

> "You would see all these founders coming in, and they all had these up and to the right graphs, whether it was sign-ups, or downloads, or MAUs. It felt to me like it wasn't obvious that those metrics that they were all getting very attached, and focused on, and showing in these presentations was the wrong thing to focus on."

The framework has three levels, and they must be addressed in order.

### Level 1: Growing Engaged Users (The Core Action)

Every consumer product has one action that, when a user completes it, signals they both understand the product and are likely to come back. This is the **core action**.

- **Facebook:** Friending
- **Pinterest:** Pinning
- **Snapchat:** Sending a snap
- **YouTube:** Subscribing

> "What you realize when you look at social products is that there almost is this action which I call the core action of that product that forms the foundation of the product. When a user completes this action, it's clear that they both understand the utility of the product... and it's an action that, if they perform the action, they're very likely to come back."

Getting clarity on the core action is critical. At Pinterest, they initially measured follows, clicks, likes, pins, repins, and time on site. They eventually converged on pinning as the core action and adopted "weekly active pinners" as their North Star.

> "We looked at every action that you could do on Pinterest... And we looked at, first of all, what percentage of users complete those actions? And if you do that action in a week, what's your propensity to come back the following week? And we basically ranked that."

The activation milestone for new users is getting them to complete the core action. If your NUX does not drive users to this action, you are leaking at the foundation.

> "Part of it is how do you measure the success of your NUX, of the activation? To me, it is are you taking those new users and helping them understand the mental model of your product well enough that they start completing the core action."

### Level 2: Retaining Users (Accruing Benefits + Mounting Loss)

Users who complete the core action once or twice and then disappear leave you with a leaky bucket. Level 2 is about making the product get better the more someone uses it, while making it harder to leave.

> "The test for me, of whether you're building a product that has the ingredients to create a retentive product on a micro level, just at the user level, is that the product should get better the more you use it, and you'll have more to lose by leaving it."

**Pinterest example:** Every pin improved the algorithmic home feed through recommendations, making the product more personalized. And each pin added to a user's boards — recipes to cook, books to read, trips to plan — creating a repository they would not want to abandon.

**Evernote example:** Every note made Evernote more valuable as a personal knowledge base. But Evernote had no loops — your usage did not make the product better for other people. That absence of Level 3 ultimately constrained them.

> "As much as I love Evernote, this is a place where Evernote obviously falls down. There's no loops that they can take advantage of, that when I use the product, I make it better for you."

### Level 3: Self-Perpetuating (Virtuous Loops)

The highest level is when engaged users create loops that pull new users into the product or make the product better for other users. This is where growth becomes organic rather than paid.

**YouTube example:** YouTube's core action evolved from uploads to subscribes. When viewers subscribed to creators, it gave creators accruing benefits (audience growth) and mounting loss (they could not leave without losing their audience). Simultaneously, viewers accumulated subscriptions that gave them personalized content — accruing benefits on the demand side too.

> "I loved that example because it showed a little bit the evolution that can happen with these companies, but then also, just the beauty of like you know you've got something really right with the core action when it's helping both sides of your network."

Products that reach Level 3 have self-sustaining growth. Products stuck at Level 2 (like Evernote) must spend money to acquire every new user.

### Where Founders Go Wrong

Sarah's most common observation: founders skip ahead. They see the full expression of Instagram or TikTok and try to build that from day one instead of nailing the core action first.

> "A lot of times, product founders, consumer founders, see where they want to get to, they compare themselves to the full expression of a product that you see with other products, ROBLOX, Instagram, TikTok, whatever it may be, and they want to get there."

> "I feel that that's the first mistake, and it's kind of natural that it would be the first mistake, but maybe the largest mistake that I see people make."

The fix: focus obsessively on Level 1. Get the core action right and get activation right before worrying about loops or growth.

## Example

At Pinterest, the team initially measured many different user actions without clarity on which one mattered most. Through quantitative analysis — looking at which actions predicted week-over-week return — they identified pinning as the core action and adopted "weekly active pinners" as the North Star metric.

For Level 2, they built the "picked for you" algorithmic feed, which was among the first algorithmic feeds in a social product. Each pin taught the algorithm more about the user, personalizing their home feed and creating accruing benefits. Users also accumulated boards full of saved content, creating mounting loss.

For Level 3, Pinterest had natural sharing loops — users would share pins and boards externally, pulling new users in. The combination of all three levels is what allowed Pinterest to grow into an enduring platform without being entirely dependent on paid acquisition.

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **Your core action** — the single action that signals an engaged user and predicts retention
2. **Activation diagnosis** — whether your NUX effectively drives users to the core action
3. **Retention mechanics** — specific accruing benefits and mounting losses built into your product
4. **Loop inventory** — which (if any) virtuous loops exist between engaged users and new users
5. **Prioritized roadmap** — which level to invest in next, based on where you are weakest
