## When to Use

Use this framework when you are building an AI product and need to decide on a pricing model. It answers the question "how should I charge?" — which Ramanujam argues is more important than "how much should I charge?" for AI companies. It is especially useful when you are navigating the shift from software-as-access (traditional SaaS) to software-as-work-delivered (AI-native).

## The Framework

The 2x2 has two axes:

- **Attribution** (x-axis): How well can you prove the value your AI delivers? Low attribution means the customer cannot clearly measure your impact. High attribution means you can point to specific metrics — reduced support tickets, increased throughput, cost savings.

- **Autonomy** (y-axis): How much does your AI operate without human involvement? Low autonomy means copilot mode — humans are still in the loop. High autonomy means the AI completes work end-to-end on its own.

> "There are two axes here. One is attribution, and the other one is autonomy. And when you have high attribution and high autonomy, that is when you have high pricing power."

### Quadrant 1: Low Attribution, Low Autonomy (Bottom-Left)
**Model: Seat-based / Subscription**

You cannot clearly attribute value and humans are still doing most of the work with AI assistance. This is classic copilot territory.

> "In that situation, the best pricing archetype that actually fits is a seat-based or a subscription model because there's not much to do about it because you're not being able to attribute a lot of value to what you bring, but you're in a copilot mode."

**Example:** Cursor and similar AI coding assistants — the developer is still writing the code, the AI assists.

**Your move:** Build more attribution. Figure out how to measure and prove your impact.

### Quadrant 2: High Attribution, Low Autonomy (Bottom-Right)
**Model: Hybrid (base fee + consumption)**

You can prove the value, but humans are still heavily involved. This enables a base subscription with usage-based components tied to measurable outcomes.

> "Those are companies that have actually done that. They can prove more attribution to what they actually bring to the table, but they are still not in a fully autonomous mode, as in there are still humans in the loop."

**Example:** Canva — base fee with consumption pricing layered on top.

**Your move:** Build toward more autonomy. What parts of the workflow can the AI handle end-to-end?

### Quadrant 3: High Autonomy, Low Attribution (Top-Left)
**Model: Usage-based / Consumption**

The AI works autonomously but you cannot clearly prove the business outcome. You charge for usage volume rather than results.

**Your move:** Build attribution. Instrument your product to measure and report the outcomes your autonomous AI is creating.

### Quadrant 4: High Attribution, High Autonomy (Top-Right) — The Golden Quadrant
**Model: Outcome-based**

This is where pricing power is maximized. The AI completes work without human involvement, and the value is clearly measurable and attributable.

> "The quadrant that you really want to be in is the golden quadrant, which is the top-right one. That's the outcome-based pricing model where you have great autonomy and great attribution. You're not only charging for work delivered, but you're charging for work delivered that was delivered by AI without no humans in the loop."

> "In the classic SaaS situation, we used to say if you can charge 10 to 20% of the value, that's actually great. But in AI, you can actually charge 25 to 50% because it is autonomous, you're doing it with the AI. There's no humans in the loop."

**Examples:** Sierra, Fin (Intercom's AI agent), Chargeflow.

### How to Use the 2x2

The framework is not just diagnostic — it is directional. Every company should be plotting a path toward the golden quadrant.

> "It's not just about figuring out where you are. It's about plotting where you need to go. Because as you build more attribution and more autonomy, you unlock more pricing power."

Ramanujam estimates only 5% of companies are currently in a true outcome-based model, but predicts that will rise to 25% within three years.

> "What this really means is if you want to win in AI, figure out a way to get to that quadrant because that's a magic quadrant."

As Lenny observed during the conversation: "This explains why everyone's pulling agents. That's where the money's at."

## Example

**Intercom's Fin product** is the clearest example of moving across quadrants. Intercom traditionally priced on a per-agent (seat-based) model — bottom-left quadrant. When they built Fin, an AI that autonomously resolves support tickets, they moved to outcome-based pricing at 99 cents per resolved ticket.

> "Traditionally, all of those kind of companies used to price based on an agent basis. How many customer service agents are actually using the product? It used to be seat based, but they built out Fin, which is a completely AI resolution for those kind of support tickets. And then that actually enables them to move to the outcome-based pricing model quadrant."

The value story is immediate: "Your agent costs 20 bucks. This thing costs 99 cents."

> "That is two chapters in one — beautifully simple pricing and an outcome-based pricing model."

**Canva** sits in the bottom-right quadrant (hybrid pricing). Ramanujam's advice to Canva: explore what you can build that creates more autonomy — an autonomous version of design creation — to unlock outcome-based pricing power.

## Output

After applying this framework, you should have:

1. **Your current quadrant** — honest assessment of where your AI product sits on attribution and autonomy
2. **Your target quadrant** — where you need to move and what you need to build to get there
3. **Your pricing model** — seat-based, hybrid, usage-based, or outcome-based, matched to your current position
4. **A value capture number** — are you capturing 10-20% of value (SaaS-era) or can you justify 25-50% (AI-era)?
5. **A migration plan** — specific product investments that increase attribution, autonomy, or both

The key insight: your pricing model is not a business decision made after the product is built. It is a product strategy decision that should shape what you build.
