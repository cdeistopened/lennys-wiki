## When to Use

Use this list when you are a new PM trying to figure out what to focus on first, a senior PM wondering where to level up, or a hiring manager looking for something more grounded than a generic career ladder. Noah wrote this as the positive counterpart to his "five myths about product management" — the things the job actually is, versus what people think it is.

> "These traits are... the things that the job actually is about. It's not a career ladder. It's not the 'Here's the structured interview things that you should interview for.'"

## The Framework

Noah Weiss, CPO of Slack, distilled the 10 traits from his experience across Google, Foursquare, and seven years leading product at Slack. They are not in a strict priority order, but they do cluster into groups that matter more at different career stages.

### 1. Live in the Future, Work Backwards

Have a longer-term vision and time horizon than anyone else on the team. Carve out time to think beyond the next sprint.

> "It's having a longer-term vision and time horizon. How do you carve out time to not just be what are we doing over the next two weeks. But six months, a year, two years from now, how do you immerse yourself in them and bring ideas back, bring inspiration back to the team."

Noah credits Paul Graham's essay on this: you do not conjure startup ideas in a park. You immerse yourself in the problem space and imagine what the future looks like, then build what is missing.

### 2. Amplify Your Team

Be a facilitator, not a dictator. Create energy, create momentum, and amplify the work that everyone else is doing.

> "A PM role I think can be a little bit unsatisfying if you're useful, where you create things yourself opposed to you are the one who's amplifying what the work that's being created by everyone else is. You have to get into that more of a facilitator mindset."

Noah is blunt about the "mini-CEO" meme: "I think that's the most dangerous piece of advice ever in the history of product management because I think that is how you end up having PMs who try to act like dictators instead of leaders and facilitators."

### 3. Facilitate the Pace and Quality of Decision-Making

Your job is not to make all the decisions. It is to help the team make high-quality decisions quickly without you playing tiebreaker.

> "How do you actually get the team to be able to make high quality decisions quickly without you arbitrarily playing tiebreaker all the time."

### 4. Execute Impeccably

This is baseline, not aspirational. No great PM is disorganized, drops follow-ups, or is unclear about timelines.

> "I've never seen a PM who was disorganized or didn't do follow-up or wasn't clear about expectations or timelines. It's not high in Maslow's hierarchy of PM enjoyment. But I do think it's a baseline expectation."

Noah invokes Ben Horowitz's rule from his Foursquare days: "Good leaders need to say what they're going to do and then do what they said. If they can't then they need to follow up and explain why."

### 5. Focus on Impact

Impact solves all PM issues. If your team is consistently building things people love and changing the direction of the business, everything else is just an input.

> "I think impact solves all PM issues, which is if a team is consistently building things people love and changing the director of the business, everything else is just an input."

### 6. Write Well

As you get more senior, writing becomes the only scalable way to have influence. Noah recommends Stephen King's *On Writing* for high-quality, high-volume production.

> "Especially as you get to more senior positions, writing is the only scalable way of having influence on a larger, larger product org."

### 7. Optimize for the Pace of Learning

Take bolder bets. Accept being wrong in the short term if it helps you discover new levers. Think of product as a portfolio, not a single bet.

> "Optimizing for learning, being willing to take those bolder bets, knowing you can be wrong in the short-term, but that you'll learn new levers that will be really fruitful in the long-term."

At Slack, this manifests as "Take bigger boulder bets. Get to the next hill to see what the horizon looks like around you."

### 8. Be the Shock Absorber

Build confidence that things will run smoothly. You are the person who absorbs the bumps so the team can focus on building.

> "You're the shock absorber for the team. You're the thing that builds people's confidence that things are going to be running smoothly."

### 9. Data Fluency

Not statistical expertise — customer fluency. Know enough about your customers from quantitative data, surveys, and direct conversations that you can make higher-likelihood product bets.

> "By data fluency, I think it's more actually... you know enough about the insights about your customers that it can then inform making higher likelihood product bets and that data can be quantitative, that data can be survey based, it can be from doing 100 meetings with customers yourself."

### 10. Great Product Taste

Develop intuition for what people will love before you can test it. Noah considers this a creative skill, not a purely analytical one.

> "What it means to have taste, which is to say you've developed some amount of intuition for what people will likely love before you're able to test it."

He recommends *Creative Selection*, about early iPhone development at Apple, as the best book on iterating creative product work.

## Priority by Career Stage

**New PMs — focus on these three:**

> "Getting great at execution. It's a thing that you can most control. Then I think building that news for impact, even if the impact is more local... and then actually do think early on getting really fluent on the data and the research side."

As Lenny adds: "Get really good at execution because that creates that aura of, 'Oh, this person's just killing it. They're just shipping on time.'"

**Senior product leaders — shift to these three:**

> "The pace and quality of decision making starts to matter a lot more because you're still responsible sometimes for teams of teams... the living in the future and working backwards... and being a really good writer, it is just the highest leverage usage of your time."

## Example

At Slack, Noah applied the "living in the future" trait through a practice called "complaint storms." The team would walk through the entire customer journey of competitor products, from landing page to first value moment, scrutinizing every friction point.

> "We'd actually start off with other products first in adjacent spaces and we'd say, 'As a group we're going to go through the customer journey from the moment you land on the website through... getting your first account going, getting the first couple of users on board, getting to the point of value.'"

This forced the team — who were also Slack power users — to see products with fresh eyes and build empathy for the next million customers who had never used Slack before.

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **A self-assessment against 10 traits** — honest evaluation of where you are strong and where you are weak
2. **Career-stage priorities** — which 2-3 traits to invest in based on your current level
3. **A shared vocabulary** — language for discussing PM performance that goes beyond generic "be more strategic" feedback
4. **Reading list** — *On Writing* by Stephen King, *Creative Selection* about Apple, and *Nobody Wants to Read Your Shit* by Steven Pressfield
