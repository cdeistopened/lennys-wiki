## When to Use

Use this framework when your team's roadmap is full of "quick wins" and incremental improvements but you suspect you are missing something bigger. It applies during quarterly or annual planning, when you are staring at a prioritized backlog and every item shows positive ROI. If your team keeps shipping features that move metrics slightly but never changes the trajectory of the business, this is the mental model shift you need.

## The Framework

The core insight: in a high-leverage role, there are hundreds of things you could do that provide positive ROI. Doing most of them is a mistake.

> "When you are in a high leverage role, you should stop doing work that simply provides a positive return on investment, ROI. And you should start focusing on work that minimizes opportunity cost."

### The Problem with ROI Thinking

The formula for ROI is: (value created - cost of your time) / cost of your time. Time is in the denominator. When you optimize for ROI, you naturally gravitate toward things that take less time — because a smaller denominator inflates the ratio.

> "How do you decrease the denominator in this case where time is the denominator? You start working on things that take less time. So you start working on the low hanging fruit. You start prioritizing the quick wins."

The result: your roadmap fills up with small, safe, well-defined tasks. Each one shows positive ROI. Each one is reasonable. And collectively, they crowd out the work that could change your business.

> "While that may be fine, in most cases, in most situations in high leverage roles, you miss the upside and you miss the opportunity that you could have gained by focusing on other things."

### The Opportunity Cost Alternative

Opportunity cost = value of the optimal option minus value of the chosen option. To minimize opportunity cost, you need to be working on the best possible thing, not just a good thing.

> "When we reprogram ourselves to think in terms of opportunity cost, we are no longer thinking, 'Oh, is this a good use of my time?' Instead, you are thinking, 'Is this the best use of my time?' And it's a subtle but profound shift in our thinking."

### Step 1: Look at Your Roadmap and Find the Ambiguous Big Bets

During planning, Shreyas consistently saw the same pattern:

> "You are trying to prioritize the next quarter. There are five sure things you can do that will have small to medium impact, and it's very clear. And then there are two ambiguous things that perhaps deep down you know you should pursue them, but you don't end up picking them because... you're satisfying yourself by observing, 'Oh, each of these is positive ROI.'"

Those ambiguous items you keep pushing to "next quarter" are exactly what you need to examine.

### Step 2: Do Not Try to Quantify Opportunity Cost

This is not about adding a column to your spreadsheet:

> "I do not recommend trying to quantify opportunity costs because it's a lost cost. Instead, what teams need is just sometimes the freedom and sometimes just permission to explore and attack these things that minimize opportunity costs."

### Step 3: Set Explicit Allocation Guidance

As a leader, give your team a budget split that forces room for big bets. Shreyas learned this from Google's classic 70-20-10 model and adapted it:

> "What I'll share as a guidance with the team is given our situation and given our strategy and given what's going on in the market, I would like us to target about 60% of our time on incrementals... 30% I want to allocate towards big new initiatives. And because it's 30%, it can't be five big new initiatives, it's probably one or two. And then 10% I'd like us to allocate towards stability and infrastructure."

The key insight about the 30% bucket: "This gives people the space to say, 'Okay, we do have this 30%, so there's no sense putting in more high ROI tasks in there or quick wins in there.'"

### Step 4: Let the Team Fill the Buckets

Once you provide the allocation, trust the team to propose what goes in each bucket:

> "I will ask the teams to create their plans and proposals based on this guidance... I often get surprised with all the awesome stuff they come back with."

## Example

At Stripe, Shreyas observed this shift firsthand. He credits Patrick Collison for modeling the opportunity cost mindset:

> "I think I learned this by just observing Patrick at Stripe, particularly over the last couple of years that I was there."

When Shreyas started working on Stripe Connect, he noticed the team was focused on incremental, positive-ROI improvements. He pushed them to consider a bigger bet:

> "I came in and it just simply instigated that like, 'Hey, how about we work on this big scary project?' Because I was hearing from customers that there is some need... of being able to manage marketplace payments in a more flexible way. And we wouldn't have looked at it if we were just focused on positive ROI, but as we started looking at it, we realized, 'Oh yes, this is a huge opportunity.'"

Stripe Connect became a major product and a large business for Stripe — one that would have been crowded out by a backlog of well-justified quick wins.

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **A reframed planning question** — from "is this a good use of our time?" to "is this the best use of our time?"
2. **An allocation split** — a concrete percentage breakdown (e.g., 60/30/10) that forces room for big bets alongside incremental work
3. **Named big bets** — the one or two ambiguous, scary initiatives that your team has been avoiding because they are hard to scope and uncertain in outcome
4. **Permission to explore** — explicit guidance from leadership that 30% (or whatever your number is) of the team's capacity is reserved for non-incremental work
5. **A check against quick-win creep** — a structural mechanism that prevents your roadmap from filling entirely with safe, small projects
