## When to Use

Use this framework when your product team keeps debating between two features of roughly equal opportunity size and nobody can break the tie. Use it when your roadmap feels like a list of good ideas rather than a coherent plan. Use it when you miss a quarterly goal and cannot explain why. The Product Strategy Stack helps you trace day-to-day prioritization problems back to their root cause — which is almost always a gap in strategic clarity somewhere above the roadmap.

Ravi Mehta developed this framework while building the Reforge product strategy program, drawing on his experience as CPO at Tinder, Product Director at Facebook, and VP of Product at Tripadvisor.

## The Framework

The Product Strategy Stack consists of five layers, each one feeding the layer below it. You can work top-down to define strategy or bottom-up to debug it.

### Layer 1: Company Mission

The change the company wants to bring to the world. A qualitative, aspirational statement of purpose. As Ravi puts it:

> "The top of the stack is the company mission and the company mission is the change the company wants to bring to the world. It's really a qualitative aspirational statement of what is the company's purpose."

Vision folds into this layer. Ravi treats mission and vision as one unit: "I always get confused about what the difference is between vision and mission. And so when I was originally working on this, there was a version of this that had the mission and the vision together... you can both describe that world and the role that the company plays in a single statement."

### Layer 2: Company Strategy

The logical plan the company will use to bring the mission into being. Unlike the mission, this must be rigorous and specific:

> "Whereas a mission is aspirational, strategy is rigorously logical. The strategy is the logical plan that your company's going to use to bring that mission into being. And so it's got to be very specific, it's got to be very rigorous."

### Layer 3: Product Strategy

The connective tissue between what the company is trying to accomplish and what the product team does every day. This is where most teams have the biggest gap. Product strategy is not a roadmap — it is the framework that informs the roadmap.

> "The product strategy is the connective tissue between what is the company trying to accomplish and what are the day-to-day things that the product team is doing."

Ravi recommends including wireframes in your strategy documentation: "Oftentimes when you talk about strategy in words alone, everyone takes away a different interpretation of that strategy, whereas when you actually can show people wireframes of what the product will look like when that strategy is implemented, it creates much more alignment."

### Layer 4: Product Roadmap

The sequence of things the team will build to execute the product strategy. This is where most companies start — and that is the mistake.

### Layer 5: Product Goals

Goals come last in the stack, not first. This is the framework's most contrarian element. Ravi argues that starting with goals (e.g., "increase retention by X%") puts the entire energy of the product team on moving numbers without any structure for understanding why:

> "The analogy I like to use, it's a little bit like taking a road trip and starting out by saying, 'Hey, we need to drive 250 miles.' It's like, no, if you're going to take a road trip, you first decide where you want to drive to. If you're in LA, you might take a road trip to Vegas. And so our destination is Vegas, and we'll know whether or not we reach there if we've driven 250 miles."

### Debugging Bottom-Up

The stack also works in reverse. If you are having trouble meeting your goals:

> "If you're having trouble meeting your goals, it might be because the roadmap isn't set up so that it can help move those goals forward. If the roadmap isn't right, it might be because the product strategy hasn't been really clearly articulated. If the product strategy isn't right, it might be because the team doesn't understand deeply enough what the company's strategy is."

## Example

Ravi walks through Tinder versus Hinge to show how different missions cascade through the entire stack despite serving the same use case.

**Mission divergence:** Hinge's mission is "designed to be deleted" — a temporary use case where success means the user leaves. Tinder's mission is "to make single life more fun" — a continuous use case where the app stays on your phone throughout your 20s and into your early 30s. As Ravi explains: "One is a temporary use case, the other is a continuous use case. And so despite the fact that they're serving the same underlying use case, which is to help people meet each other, they have very different missions."

**Product strategy divergence:** Tinder was the original swipe-based dating app, built to be lightweight and serendipitous. Hinge deliberately rejected swipe mechanics: "They wanted people to spend more time on each other's profiles. They wanted to create more tools for those profiles. So Tinder profiles are very simple. Hinge profiles have prongs."

**Roadmap convergence and divergence:** Both invested heavily in video chat post-pandemic (convergence), but Tinder resisted adding search filters that Hinge embraced (divergence). The filter decision traces directly to product strategy: "From a product philosophy standpoint, they wanted people to get to know each other and chat rather than to feel like Tinder's a search engine for people."

**Goals convergence:** Both measure success through meaningful conversations — matches leading to chats. But the product mechanics that drive those conversations are completely different because the layers above are different.

## Output

After applying the Product Strategy Stack, you walk away with:

1. **A completed five-layer document** — mission, company strategy, product strategy, roadmap, and goals, each clearly separated
2. **A diagnostic of where your gaps are** — most prioritization struggles trace to a missing or vague layer above the roadmap
3. **A tie-breaking framework** — when two features look equal, the product strategy layer tells you which one is aligned
4. **Wireframes attached to strategy** — visual representations that prevent divergent interpretations
5. **Goals that are contextualized** — metrics that measure progress toward a destination, not arbitrary targets
