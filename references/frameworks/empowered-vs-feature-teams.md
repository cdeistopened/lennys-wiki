## When to Use

Use this diagnostic when you suspect your product team is not actually doing product management — just project management with a fancier title. Use it when engineers and designers on your team question the value of having a PM. Use it when your roadmap is a list of features handed down from executives or big-pocket customers rather than problems to solve. Use it when you want to assess whether your current role is building real PM skills or creating a resume gap that will be exposed at a stronger company.

Marty Cagan developed this diagnostic across decades of working with product companies through Silicon Valley Product Group. He is the author of INSPIRED, EMPOWERED, and TRANSFORMED.

## The Framework

### The Spectrum

Product teams exist on a spectrum from delivery team to empowered product team. Most teams land somewhere in the middle — the feature team — and mistakenly believe they are doing product management.

**Product Owner (Delivery Team):** The extreme end. This person learned backlog management in a certification class and their job is essentially administering Jira. Marty is blunt about this role:

> "What they learned in a CSPO or a PSPO class was how to manage a backlog in Jira, which to me is very analogous to learning how to operate Google Docs. Of course, that's not the job."

**Feature Team Product Manager:** A step up from product owner. This person does project management — coordinating timelines, writing specs, herding stakeholders. But they are not responsible for whether the product actually delivers value:

> "A feature team product manager is somewhere in between there. They do more than administer the backlog. They do a lot of project management. And don't get me wrong, project management is important, but it is not product management."

**Empowered Product Manager:** The real role. This person is a creator, not a facilitator. They are responsible for value (does the customer want this?) and viability (does it work for the business?):

> "On a real empowered product team, product manager is a creator, not a facilitator. I always cringe when somebody tells me, oh, my job is to say why? And I'm like, 'Well, what do you do for the rest of the week besides the 10 minutes it takes you to say why?'"

### The Diagnostic Questions

**1. What are you given?**

Feature teams are given a roadmap of output — features and projects to design, build, test, and deploy. Empowered teams are given problems to solve:

> "On a feature team, you're basically given a roadmap of output. That's the key, is output... And a product team, an empowered product team, instead of being given that roadmap of features, they're given problems to solve. Now they're customer problems or they're business problems or both."

**2. What is measured?**

Feature teams are measured on shipping. Empowered teams are measured on outcomes:

> "The measure is not ship the thing. The measure is it solves the problem. And that's why really the biggest difference between a strong product company and the rest is strong product companies understand it's all about outcomes."

Marty frames this in language that resonates with executives: "It's about time to money more than time to market. We know how to do time to market. If you insist on time to market, we know how to do that. The techniques are well-known. The harder part is time to money."

**3. What are you responsible for?**

On a feature team, the PM covers usability and feasibility — did we build what was asked and does it work? On an empowered team, the PM is responsible for value and viability:

> "It's only when you sign up for an outcome that you have the needs for a product manager... Because if you've been asked to solve these problems, that means you have to come up with a solution that's not only usable and feasible, which is what a feature team does, but is also valuable and viable."

**4. Do engineers question the PM's value?**

This is the biggest clue. If engineers and designers say they do not need a PM, you are almost certainly on a feature team:

> "They don't need PMs in a feature team. They don't because it's a project management role, and they already have plenty of people who can cover that. And furthermore, a lot of times the engineers or the designers say, 'We'd rather do it ourselves than deal with this person.'"

**5. Where does strategy come from?**

In good product companies, leaders do the product strategy and teams figure out solutions. This is not top-down in the feature-factory sense — it is leaders doing their job:

> "Product teams don't do product strategy. Product leaders do product strategy. They need to do the product strategy... But then in a good organization, you give those bets to the teams and you really do give them latitude to figure it out."

### The Skills an Empowered PM Needs

If you want to move from feature team to empowered team, these are the capabilities Marty says you must build:

- **Deep customer expertise.** Marty's coach would not let him take the PM role until he had visited 30 customers in person: "I was not allowed to take the product manager role until I had visited 30 customers in person, 15 in the US, 15 in Europe."
- **Data expertise.** How is the product being used? How is usage changing?
- **Business constraint knowledge.** Compliance, legal, sales, marketing, monetization, go-to-market — all of this is the PM's domain.
- **Market understanding.** What competitors are doing, where the market is heading.

Without this person on the team, the alternative is design by committee: "If you don't have this person on the team and you want to empower this team to make decisions, what are you going to do? You're just going to make it up? Or what they usually do is they call meetings with 20 stakeholders all in a room to try to decide these things."

### What You Can Do About It

Marty rejects the idea that feature-team PMs are stuck: "Too many people in our industry view themselves as a victim of their company, like they're stuck in a feature team and there's nothing they can do about it other than quit. I think that's not true. There is so much they can do."

His advice: do a self-assessment, raise your skills, and demonstrate the difference. At minimum, your company will notice and promote you. At best, leadership will say "let's try running a set of teams this way and see how we do."

## Example

Marty contrasts B2B sales-driven companies with product-led companies. Oracle and SAP are massively valuable but sales-driven — and their products are widely disliked. The best B2B companies like Trainline (highlighted in TRANSFORMED) moved from feature-team models to empowered teams and saw dramatic improvements. In TRANSFORMED, Marty profiles companies outside Silicon Valley — in healthcare, financial services, travel, and fitness — that successfully made this transition. A financial services company moved from sales-driven feature delivery to empowered product teams and "dramatically improved things for the sales organization" as a result.

The key insight is that an empowered product team can do everything a feature team can do, plus more. The reverse is not true.

## Output

After applying this diagnostic, you walk away with:

1. **A clear classification** of where your team sits on the spectrum — delivery team, feature team, or empowered product team
2. **A skills gap assessment** — which value and viability skills do you personally need to build
3. **A conversation starter** — concrete language to explain to leadership why the current model is leaving value on the table
4. **An action plan** — whether to raise your game in place, propose a pilot team, or seek a company that works differently
5. **A filter for career decisions** — when evaluating your next role, research the hiring manager and assess whether they run empowered teams or feature teams
