## When to Use

Use this framework when you need to define what product management actually is at your company, set up career ladders, evaluate PM candidates, give feedback to PMs on your team, or assess your own strengths and gaps. It works for APMs through CPOs — the 12 competencies stay the same, but how they manifest changes with seniority. If your PM feedback conversations feel surface-level or you are struggling to articulate what "good" looks like for your PM team, start here.

## The Framework

Ravi Mehta developed this model at Tripadvisor when the company needed to rapidly hire PMs and build a rotational program. The goal was to clearly define what skills a PM needs so they could train people into the role from scratch.

> "We really needed to define very clearly what is product management and how do we help people identify the skills that they need to be an effective product manager and give them a plan so that they can grow those skills."

As Lenny puts it: "This is an incredible framework. I've never found a simpler, more beautiful, very clear, easy to consume and share version of what the PM role is."

The model consists of **12 competencies organized into 4 pillars**. All 12 matter at every level of seniority.

### Pillar 1: Product Execution

The foundation. Can you work with your team to build product?

1. **Functional Specification** — Defining what to build. Writing the PRD or spec that captures the requirements.
2. **Product Delivery** — Working with engineering and design to turn that spec into working product.
3. **Product Quality** — Ensuring what you build is high quality across technical, design, usability, and business dimensions.

> "Ultimately that's the foundation of being a successful product manager is being able to execute. And that's as true for an APM as it is for a VP or a CPO."

At the APM level, this means day-to-day individual contribution. At the CPO level, it means building the systems that enable teams to specify, deliver, and ship quality products.

### Pillar 2: Customer Insight

Understanding customers so you know what to build.

4. **Fluency with Data** — Using quantitative and qualitative data at your fingertips to make decisions about customer needs.
5. **Voice of the Customer** — Having conversations with customers so you can advocate for them throughout the company.
6. **User Experience Design** — Thinking about how functionality translates into user experience (not UI design — this applies to APIs, ML models, and any product surface).

> "A fundamental part of being a really good product manager is the ability to think about the user experience in a very detailed way to make sure that you're not just defining functionality, but you're really clearly understanding how that functionality turns into user experience."

### Pillar 3: Product Strategy

Connecting your work to business outcomes.

7. **Business Outcome Ownership** — Understanding how your product drives value for the business, not just shipping features.
8. **Product Vision and Roadmapping** — Assembling individual pieces of work into a coherent vision and roadmap that serves the company strategy.
9. **Strategic Impact** — Sequencing business outcomes to move the strategy forward. Think of this as a roadmap of outcomes, not features.

> "It's really important to move away from thinking about product as shipping features to driving business outcomes."

### Pillar 4: Influencing People

Leading without always having direct authority.

10. **Stakeholder Inclusion** — Working with people across the organization to rally them around your work.
11. **Team Leadership** — Helping direct reports become great PMs (relevant once you manage people).
12. **Managing Up** — Winning the support of leadership for your direction.

### How to Use It: The Self-Assessment

Rate yourself on each competency as **needs focus**, **on track**, or **outperforming**. Then ask your manager to do the same exercise independently. The areas where you agree and disagree become the stimulus for a deep conversation.

> "Go through each competency and rate themselves needs focus, on track or outperforming on each of the competencies to quickly get a read on where you feel like you're landing. You can ask your manager to do that same thing. You can do that in five or 10 minutes."

### Exponential Feedback

Ravi calls this the concept behind the model's real power. Surface-level feedback fixes a symptom once and it is gone. Competency-based feedback has compounding returns because it helps people diagnose their own performance.

> "If you give someone feedback on a particular symptom or you give them feedback on something that's tactical and they fix that in a moment, the feedback, the conclusion of that feedback, it just happens and then it's gone. But if instead you help a person understand the underlying behaviors that led to that particular situation, then they can focus on growing themselves."

## Example

At Tripadvisor, Ravi's team created a Product Rotational Program that hired people directly out of business school and undergrad — regardless of prior PM experience — into four six-month rotations across zero-to-one teams, growth teams, and infrastructure teams.

> "We would hire people directly out of business school and out of undergrad into their first product role regardless of whether or not they had prior product experience. And they would go through two years of rotations... the goal was in about two years to get a person to be able to experience various different parts of product management."

The competency model provided the structure for coaching these new PMs throughout their rotations. It told them exactly what skills to develop, let their managers give targeted feedback, and helped them map their natural strengths to the right type of PM role (growth PM, product discovery PM, etc.).

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **A 12-point self-assessment** — each competency rated as needs focus, on track, or outperforming
2. **A manager comparison** — the same assessment from your manager, revealing alignment gaps
3. **Growth priorities** — 2-3 specific competencies to develop next
4. **PM archetype clarity** — understanding which clusters of competencies define your style (growth PM, innovation PM, etc.)
5. **A feedback language** — a shared vocabulary for giving and receiving PM feedback that gets to root causes, not symptoms
6. **Career ladder foundation** — a structure for defining PM levels at your company
