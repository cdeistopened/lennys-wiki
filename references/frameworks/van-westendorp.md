## When to Use

Use this framework when you are setting or revisiting the price for your product and you do not know where to start. It works best when you already have clarity on your positioning — you know whether you are serving the high end, mid-market, or mass market. The Van Westendorp method is one of the simplest quantitative approaches to pricing, requiring only four survey questions asked to roughly 100 users. Rahul Vohra, founder and CEO of Superhuman, used it to set their $30/month price point for email software — a price that seemed audacious for a category where alternatives were free.

## The Framework

The core principle: pricing follows positioning, never the other way around.

> "I always say the same thing when it comes to pricing, which is before you figure out pricing, you must first figure out positioning."

### Step 1: Establish Your Positioning

Before you run the pricing exercise, know where you sit in the market. Are you best-in-class? Budget-friendly? Niche? Your price must support that position.

Superhuman's positioning was clear: the best email tool on the market, built for high-performing teams. They had the metrics to back it up — users got through email twice as fast, responded one to two days faster, and saved four hours per week.

> "We're very confident in saying that Superhuman is the best email tool on the market and that we're building it for high performing teams and high performing individuals. In other words, we serve the high end of the market."

### Step 2: Ask Four Questions

Survey roughly 100 of your earliest users with these four questions:

1. **Too expensive:** At what price would you consider the product so expensive that you would not consider buying it?
2. **Too cheap:** At what price would you consider the product priced so low that you would worry about its quality and would not buy it?
3. **Getting expensive:** At what price would you consider the product starting to get expensive — not out of the question, but you would have to give some thought to buying it?
4. **A bargain:** At what price would you consider the product a bargain — a great buy for the money?

> "We asked, I think it was around a hundred of our earliest users, the following four questions."

### Step 3: Choose the Right Price Point for Your Positioning

This is where most startups go wrong. The natural instinct is to orient around price point 4 — the bargain price. That makes sense for greenfield opportunities and marketplaces where you want maximum top-of-funnel volume.

But if you are positioning as best-in-class, orient around price point 3 — the "getting expensive" answer.

> "Most startups orient around price point number four. This is especially true for greenfield opportunities, marketplaces, you've got to set the transaction value around price point four. Basically when you want as many people to sign up as is humanly possible, at the top of the funnel. But the price point that supports our best in class, best in category position, is actually the third one."

The logic: at this price, customers pause and think about the value. Then they do the ROI math and buy anyway. That deliberation is the signal that the price is working.

> "It starts to feel expensive, but then you sit down and you think about the time that you spend in email, the ROI, and you still buy it anyway."

### Step 4: Gut-Check Against Market Size

Once you have a price, do the basic math to make sure it supports your business goals.

> "Once we picked our price, we then do a quick gut check on market size. For example... our ARR would have to be a hundred million dollars. Well, that would be 300,000 subscribers at $30 per month... do we think we can get to hundreds of thousands of subscribers? And we answered emphatically, yes."

This is a back-of-envelope sanity check, not a precise model. The point is to make sure the price is not structurally incompatible with the scale of business you are trying to build.

## Example

Superhuman needed to price an email client in a market where Gmail and Outlook were free. Their positioning as the premium, best-in-class tool meant they could not use the bargain price.

They surveyed roughly 100 early users with the four Van Westendorp questions. The median answer for question 3 — "starting to get expensive, but you'd still consider it" — was $30 per month. That became the price.

The gut check: at $30/month, they would need 300,000 subscribers to reach $100M ARR (assuming 10x revenue as valuation). They were confident that was achievable even without price increases or up-market expansion. In fact, they have since raised prices and expanded into enterprise, validating that the initial price left room to grow.

Rahul credits Madhavan Ramanujam's book *Monetizing Innovation* as the source for the broader pricing methodology, with Van Westendorp being one of the easiest methods described in it.

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **Four data distributions** — one for each pricing question, from ~100 respondents
2. **A price point matched to your positioning** — question 3 for premium, question 4 for mass market
3. **A market-size gut check** — confirmation that the price supports your revenue and valuation goals
4. **Pricing confidence** — a data-backed answer to "why do you charge X?" instead of guesswork
5. **A reusable method** — you can rerun this survey as your market position or customer base evolves
