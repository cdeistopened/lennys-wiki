## When to Use

Use this framework when your team knows they should be talking to customers but it keeps falling off the calendar. It applies equally to teams that have never interviewed a customer and teams that run quarterly research sprints but go months in between. Torres frames the core problem: "Digital products are never done. It's not like the Netflix team is going to show up to work one day and be like, 'Hey, our product's good enough.' We're always iterating, we're always improving. Customer needs are always evolving." If your product keeps evolving, your understanding of customers must too — and that means building interviews into a weekly habit, not a project phase.

## The Framework

### Step 1: Accept the Continuous Mindset

Stop thinking of discovery as a phase that precedes delivery. Torres is blunt: "People make this mistake — they think about it as phases. First I discover and then I deliver. No, you're always delivering and you're always discovering. The more you build this discovery habit, the better those bets are going to get with time."

The minimum viable version is surprisingly small: "You can do it in as little as an interview a week, on the interviewing side, on the discovering opportunity side."

### Step 2: Automate Recruiting

The biggest barrier to weekly interviews is the effort of finding someone to talk to. Torres designed her system after reading Nudge by Thaler and Sunstein: "I started thinking about this in the context of interviewing. I want to see product teams interview every week. So how do I make it easier for them to do that than to not do that?"

The goal is zero-effort scheduling: "Can I make it so that when you wake up on Monday morning, there's an interview on your calendar and you literally did nothing to get it there?"

The mechanism varies by company. For B2B, she works with customer-facing teams: "I just have teams define a trigger every week, like, 'Hey, this week we're looking to talk to somebody who's experiencing this need or pain point. If you happen to be on the phone with someone who's experiencing that, just go ahead and use scheduling software, put it on our calendar.' The goal is for the product team to not be involved at all. They literally just have to show up and connect the interview."

For consumer products, Torres pushes teams past the "I'm not allowed to talk to customers" objection: "Your company doesn't own you when you're not at work. I bet you know people like your customers. Why don't you just start there?"

### Step 3: Collect Stories, Not Opinions

The single most important interviewing skill is getting customers to tell stories about real past behavior rather than speculating about what they would do. Torres warns: "Product people are in the business of changing behavior, understanding and changing behavior. I think that's a really big mistake that teams make — both in their prototype tests and in their interviews, they focus on what people would do, on what people think, on why they think they do something. It's all really unreliable. It's a garbage in, garbage out situation. The real measure is tell me about your behavior. What did you actually do?"

### Step 4: Ask One Question and Follow the Timeline

Throw out the 50-question interview protocol. Torres explains: "So many people write these who, why, how, 50-question long interview protocols. It leads to a cadence of the interview that is not a natural conversation. The first thing to remember is that you're just talking to a human."

Her method is radically simple: "You really don't have to think about what to ask. You could run an entire interview by asking them one question." She demonstrates by asking Lenny to tell her about the last time he watched streaming entertainment, then follows the thread: "I literally could continue this entire interview by just saying, 'Oh, you opened Netflix. What happened next? Oh, you didn't find anything. How come?' All I have to do is just be curious about your experience. What I'm doing with my questions is just helping you tell the timeline."

### Step 5: Listen Instead of Planning the Next Question

Torres identifies the core failure mode: "One of the reasons why we get bad at interviewing, we're so worried about asking the next question, we stop listening to the interviewee. We just missed everything we were told. We missed those moments of like, 'Oh, there is some friction. You couldn't find something to watch. Tell me about that.'"

### Step 6: Watch for the Sign of a Good Interview

Torres offers a concrete quality check: "The sign that you ran a good interview is your customer is going to say, 'Wow. When can we do this again?'" This happens naturally when you collect stories rather than interrogate. "If you collect stories in your interviews, customers love it."

## Example

Torres shares a story about a healthcare product team that builds badges for hospital workstations. The team spent weeks claiming they could not find a customer to talk to. Torres pushed back, pointing out that hospitals were everywhere around them. The barrier was not access — it was the team overthinking what "proper channels" looked like. Once they stopped waiting for permission and simply found a nurse to talk to, the interviews started flowing.

Torres also notes that even individual contributors in rigid organizations can build the habit: "I'm always amazed at how much I could do by just ignoring everybody around me and how they were working and finding a way to do it."

## Output

After implementing continuous discovery interviews, you walk away with:

1. **A weekly recurring interview** on the calendar that fills itself through automated recruiting
2. **Rich customer stories** about real behavior, not hypothetical preferences
3. **A growing opportunity space** that feeds your Opportunity Solution Tree with real pain points and desires
4. **A sustainable habit** — Torres emphasizes that at minimum one hour per week (the interview itself) is the entire time commitment for the interviewing side
5. **Assumption testing fuel** — stories surface the specific assumptions you need to validate about your candidate solutions
