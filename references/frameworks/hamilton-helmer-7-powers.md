## When to Use

Use this framework when you are trying to answer "What is our moat?" and want a rigorous answer instead of a hand-wavy one. It is useful for founders writing their strategy slide, product leaders evaluating competitive positioning, or anyone who has been told to "think more strategically." Hamilton Helmer spent decades as a strategy advisor and investor distilling business strategy down to one question: do you have power? If you do not, no amount of operational excellence will save you.

> "Power requires a benefit and a barrier, so he's taking care of the benefit part by saying a castle, you have to have a pretty good understanding of why it's a castle and not a shack."

## The Framework

### The Core Concept: Power = Benefit + Barrier

A power gives you either a cost or price advantage over competitors (the benefit), and there is something durable that prevents competitors from copying it (the barrier). Both must be present and both must be material — a penny of advantage is not power.

> "You have to say, you have to understand how competition takes place and say what is it that creates some kind of refuge? And what it is is there's something in what you do that gives you either a cost or price advantage over others... And the barrier side is that there's something that is durable about that that makes it over time that you can't, the competitors can't take away from you."

Strategy, in Helmer's narrowed definition, is about the fundamental determinants of business value. It is a long-time-horizon concept — tactical wins are not strategy.

> "Think of Pearl Harbor, Pearl Harbor for the Japanese was this enormous tactical success... the worst possible strategic move because it completely solved Franklin Delano Roosevelt's problem of how he could get the US citizens on board to attacking Hitler."

### The Seven Powers

1. **Scale Economies** — More volume means lower cost per unit due to fixed cost amortization. Netflix spreads content costs across more subscribers than competitors can.

2. **Network Economies** — The product becomes more valuable as more people use it. Helmer distinguishes this from "network effects" — many products have network effects, but only those where the value difference is material enough to sustain a real price or cost advantage have network economies as a power.

> "There are lots of things that I would say have network effects but not network economies."

> "You asked me earlier... about Uber and Lyft, I'd say that they probably have network effects involved but not network economies."

3. **Counter Positioning** — A newcomer adopts a business model that an incumbent cannot copy without damaging their existing business. This is the most accessible power for startups because it is available from day one.

4. **Switching Costs** — Customers face real costs (time, money, learning) if they move to a competitor.

5. **Branding** — Durable brand value that commands a price premium. Not brand recognition (you can buy that with an ad), but deep brand association that takes years to build.

6. **Cornered Resource** — You own or control something valuable that would benefit a competitor if they had it, but they cannot get it. The barrier is often legal (patents, licenses) or informational.

7. **Process Power** — Operational excellence so deeply embedded in the organization that competitors cannot replicate it. Toyota's production system is the canonical example.

### Power Progression: What Is Available When

Not all powers are available at every stage. Helmer's concept of "power progression" tells you which ones to focus on based on where your business is in its lifecycle.

**For early-stage startups, remove three from the list:**

> "If you're starting a company, take those off the table. So those two are branding and process power... take branding and process power... So take those off the table and then a resource type of power, which is... usually not the types of things that I'm dealing with and it's obvious to everybody."

**That leaves four powers for startups to pursue:**

1. **Counter positioning** — Available immediately. Start here. Your novel business model or approach is something the incumbent cannot adopt without self-harm.
2. **Scale economies** — Develop as you grow.
3. **Switching costs** — Develop as you accumulate customers.
4. **Network economies** — Develop as your user base reaches critical mass.

> "Almost every startup that you want to deal with starts with counter positioning because remember what product market fit is primarily is a substitution... and that substitution, so your competition at that point is functional competition."

### Common Delusions

**"We have a data moat."** Possible but rare. The problem is that the advantage curve flattens — beyond a certain scale, more data yields diminishing returns in cost advantage.

> "People often think that they get scale economies through data. And I'd say that that's possible, but it's rare."

**"We have a flywheel."** Maybe, but is it material? Helmer's team is skeptical every time they hear this word.

> "We laugh whenever we hear somebody say they have a flywheel... There are often flywheels, the ones that really are material, are rare. The key thing here is materiality, not whether the flywheel exists, but whether the effect is strong enough to really tilt returns."

**"We execute faster than everyone."** That is operational excellence, not power. If you stop running, you get caught.

> "You're on a treadmill and if you stop running on that treadmill, you get creamed, but it's not power. The things that drive operational excellence can be mimicked."

### When to Start Thinking About Power

Helmer's view has evolved. He used to believe strategy came after product-market fit. Now he believes founders should think about power from the very beginning — not in the form of detailed strategic plans, but by asking which of their business propositions tilt toward the eventual availability of power.

> "When should you be thinking about this? The answer is always."

### The Three Determinants of Value

Everything that creates value in a company falls into three categories: power, market size, and operational excellence.

> "So I'd say they're right and I'm right. They're right because there are this incredible... I mean business is really hard and there are just a multitude of things you have to pay attention for. I'm right because all those things fall into those three categories."

## Example

Netflix demonstrates scale economies clearly. Their content budget is a massive fixed cost — roughly 50% of their cost structure. With more subscribers than any competitor, they can spread that cost more thinly.

> "Netflix with scale economies, they have more subscribers. The cost of their content is a very large fixed cost, about 50% of their cost structure every year. They can take that fixed cost and spread it over more subscribers so their cost per subscriber is less."

Uber and Lyft, by contrast, illustrate the difference between network effects and network economies. Both have network effects (more drivers = shorter wait times), but the advantage is not material enough to prevent aggressive competition.

> "The advantage that they get, it's not material."

Helmer also uses the heuristic for process power: if you cannot write down what makes your operations superior, and no consulting firm offers to replicate it for competitors, you might have genuine process power. Toyota is the rare real example.

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **Power audit** — which of the seven powers you currently have (if any), with honest assessment of materiality
2. **Power progression map** — which powers are available to you at your current stage
3. **Counter positioning check** — whether your business model creates a real dilemma for incumbents
4. **Delusion filter** — honest evaluation of whether your "moat" is real or wishful thinking
5. **Strategic focus** — clarity on which power to pursue and what must be true for it to materialize
