## When to Use

Use this framework when your leadership team is misaligned on priorities — when the CEO wants growth, the CFO wants monetization, and product wants to invest in quality. It works at any stage but is especially valuable at inflection points: transitioning from startup to scale-up, navigating a growth slowdown, or deciding whether to invest in a new revenue stream versus building a better product. Gibson Biddle calls this "the number one source of misalignment that I discover among startups."

## The Framework

GEM stands for Growth, Engagement, and Monetization. JAM is the prioritized version — the rank order your company agrees to right now. The model comes from Biddle's experience as VP of Product at Netflix, where aligning the co-founders on a single priority order unlocked clearer decision-making across the entire organization.

### Step 1: Define the Three Levers

**Growth** — Year-over-year customer growth. How fast are you acquiring new users or subscribers? Are you targeting 10% or 50% growth?

**Engagement** — Product quality, measured through a proxy metric. A more engaging product is a better product.

> "Engagement is how I think about product quality. A more engaging product is a better product. So if you think investing in building a better product is super important, then you go with engagement, and the proxy metric that I use for that at Netflix was retention."

**Monetization** — How much effort do you put into turning your users and product into a business? Pricing, packaging, revenue per user.

> "You have these customers, you have this product, and how much effort do you put into turning that into a business?"

### Step 2: Force-Rank Them

Get your leadership team in a room and force-rank the three factors. No ties allowed.

Biddle tells the story of Netflix's two co-founders:

> "Dan said, 'Well, that's easy. Growth, engagement, and monetization.' And Greg said, 'Yeah, that's easy. Monetization, engagement, and growth.' He did the flip, right? I'm like, 'I'm going to come back in two hours. You guys got to fight this out.'"

The disagreement is the point. Surfacing it is what creates alignment. Leaving it unresolved is what creates organizational drift.

> "This is really the number one source of misalignment that I discover among startups. And startups, you're always flipping back and forth between growth and engagement. Grow faster, build a better product. Grow faster, and then later, you get a little later to monetization."

### Step 3: Start with a SWAG

Before facilitating the group discussion, the product leader should form their own point of view first.

> "Personally, I'd start with a SWAG. I would take a shot, a stupid, wild-ass guess of what I think is right, so at least I started with a point of view before I shared it with my team."

### Step 4: Define Your Engagement Metric

This is the hardest conversation. Growth is usually straightforward (year-over-year customer growth rate). Monetization is measurable (revenue, ARPU). But engagement requires choosing a proxy that the whole company can rally around.

> "The hardest one is, what is your engagement metric? How do you measure product quality? At Netflix, it's monthly retention, but that's a hard conversation."

### Step 5: Reassess Regularly

The rank order is not permanent. As context changes — growth stalls, competition increases, you raise a new round — the priorities may shift.

The GEM/JAM model connects directly to Biddle's DHM (Delight, Hard-to-copy, Margin-enhancing) strategy model. Every product initiative should delight customers in hard-to-copy, margin-enhancing ways. But when you have limited resources, GEM/JAM tells you which of those three outcomes to weight most heavily in your prioritization.

## Example

At Netflix, Biddle used GEM to resolve a real disagreement between co-founders. The debate was not abstract — it determined how the product team allocated engineering resources, which features got built, and which metrics the company optimized for.

The DHM model gave Netflix a language for evaluating any strategic decision. When Netflix considered auto-canceling users who were paying but not watching, the team ran it through DHM:

> "Is this a delightful thing to do?" Yes — customers who find out love the gesture.

> "What's the hard to copy advantage?" Brand trust.

> "The bad news, you're going to lose a hundred million bucks."

Netflix chose to auto-cancel — the delight and hard-to-copy advantage outweighed the margin hit, because at that moment their GEM rank was Growth first, Engagement second, Monetization third. A company prioritizing Monetization first would have made a different call.

When Netflix later lost customers for the first time in Q1 2022, the context changed. The team evaluated an ad-supported lower-priced tier through DHM:

> "Would an ad-supported plan that's lower price be delightful for customers? For a set of customers who are ad tolerant, that might be a reasonable trade off. Would Netflix be building hard to copy advantage if they did this?"

The strategic question shifted because the GEM rank had shifted — growth was now urgent in a way it had not been before.

Biddle also shared how the JAM model applies to team size as a forcing function:

> "If you want to be a muckety muck, my theory is the skills of a leader are the same, whether you're head of product, or the chief financial officer, or head of data."

The GEM/JAM alignment determines not just what teams build but how leaders communicate the vision across the organization.

## Output

After applying this framework, you should have:

1. **A force-ranked priority order** — Growth, Engagement, or Monetization, agreed upon by your leadership team
2. **A defined engagement metric** — the proxy you will use to measure product quality (retention, DAU/MAU, session depth, etc.)
3. **A decision-making filter** — when evaluating any initiative, weight the three factors according to your current JAM order
4. **An alignment test** — ask each leader independently to rank GEM; if they disagree, you have found your most important leadership conversation
5. **A reassessment cadence** — when to revisit the rank order based on changing context (fundraise, growth slowdown, competitive pressure)
