## When to Use

Use this framework when you are overwhelmed with work, when your to-do list never seems to shrink, or when you find yourself spending equal effort on every task regardless of its importance. It is particularly useful for PMs, founders, and anyone in a creative high-leverage role where perfectionism becomes a trap. If you are working long hours but still feel dissatisfied with your output, this framework is for you.

## The Framework

The core insight is that not all tasks are created equal, but ambitious people tend to treat them all the same way. Shreyas discovered this during his early years at Google:

> "When I just joined Google as a relatively new PM, this is back in 2008, for the first three years, I was overwhelmed and stressed... my to-do list was endless and I wasn't able to make a dent on it, and I was also a little bit of a perfectionist."

### Step 1: Classify Every Task

Look at your to-do list and label each item as L, N, or O:

**L — Leverage tasks.** These return 10X or 100X the effort you put in.

> "The L tasks are such that when you put in a certain amount of effort, you get 10X or 100X in return in terms of impact."

**N — Neutral tasks.** You get roughly what you put in — 1X input for about 1.1X output.

> "Those are tasks where you basically get what you put in, or just a little more than that."

**O — Overhead tasks.** You get back far less impact than the effort required.

> "There are overhead tasks where, again, in terms of impact, you get back a lot less than you actually put in."

### Step 2: Recognize That the Same Activity Can Be Any Category

This is the non-obvious part. Filing a bug report, taking meeting notes, writing a PRD — any of these can be an L, N, or O task depending on context.

Shreyas uses note-taking as an example:

> "If it was a product review with the CEO about a very contentious topic that you have gone back and forth multiple times, and now you made a decision about something, you want to perfect those notes before you send them out, you want to get the language right, you want to be very clear on what the decision is, so there's no room for misinterpretation... That's a case where it's an L task. And I would say just spend an hour or even two hours perfecting those notes."

Contrast that with routine meeting notes: "People need to really know is the three action items that came out of the meetings who owns them, that's it."

### Step 3: Match Your Energy to the Task Type

- **For L tasks**: Let your inner perfectionist shine. Do them during your most productive hours. Spend more time than you normally would.
- **For N tasks**: Do them competently but quickly. Do not over-invest.
- **For O tasks**: Get them done with minimum viable effort. An expense report does not need to be polished.

> "For the L tasks, let my inner perfectionist shine because I'm going to get so much more in return. It makes sense for me to spend that time on that PRD, for instance, related to an important feature that will meaningfully impact our revenue."

### Step 4: Identify Your Procrastinated L Tasks

The tasks you are avoiding are almost certainly your L tasks. Shreyas is direct about why:

> "The reason we procrastinate on these tasks are, one, because we know that they're L tasks, we know the impact they'll have, and we are a little scared... Why many people procrastinate on strategy? Because deep down, they don't know if they can formulate a good strategy. So time becomes a convenient excuse."

The classic tell: "I know I need to work on getting our strategy right, but I don't have time because I'm busy firefighting."

### Step 5: Use Tactical Tricks to Attack L Tasks

Shreyas recommends two specific tactics:

**Placebo productivity.** Intentionally fill a day or two with N and O tasks while consciously labeling them as such. This builds psychological momentum:

> "I intentionally do N tasks and O tasks. I fill up my day with N and O tasks. And I keep reminding myself, 'Yeah, you're just doing neutral and overhead tasks.' Because then that just tricks me into thinking, 'Okay, if I've been doing this placebo productivity task for the last two days, now, it's the right time for me to do this L task.'"

**Change of location.** Work from a different physical space when tackling an L task:

> "Nothing, for me, at least fights my procrastination for L tasks better than changing the place from which I'm working... I find that change of place just forces a focus and a shift in mindset that helps me bang out that L task very quickly and do it really well."

## Example

At Google, Shreyas was spending equal effort on every task — from expense reports to strategic PRDs. He was working long hours but felt no progress. After discovering the LNO concept:

> "What I realized is that among the things in my to-do list that are actually only very few L tasks, and so, it made sense for me to focus a lot on those L tasks, to take on those L tasks when I was feeling most productive, most energetic during a certain time of the day."

The time freed up by doing N and O tasks faster went directly into the L tasks that moved the needle. The same number of hours, radically different output.

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **A labeled task list** — every item on your to-do list classified as L, N, or O
2. **A time reallocation plan** — more hours on L tasks, fewer on N and O tasks
3. **Identified procrastination patterns** — the L tasks you have been avoiding, now named and scheduled
4. **A default effort level for each category** — perfectionism on L tasks, competence on N tasks, minimum viable on O tasks
5. **Tactical calendar blocks** — L tasks scheduled during peak energy, at a different location if needed

As Lenny observes: "The high leverage tasks being the task that you know you should do, but don't want to do... wherever the compass is pointing where it's most difficult, it's probably where the biggest opportunity lies."
