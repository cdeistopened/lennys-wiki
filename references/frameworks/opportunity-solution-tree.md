## When to Use

Use the Opportunity Solution Tree when your team has been given an outcome (engagement, retention, revenue) but needs to figure out what to build to move it. It is especially valuable when stakeholders keep prescribing features, when the team is stuck in output mode instead of outcome mode, or when there are too many possible directions and no structured way to evaluate them. As Torres puts it: "Most product teams don't really know how to manage this really complex problem of how do I start from an outcome and figure out what to build."

## The Framework

The Opportunity Solution Tree (OST) is a tree diagram that connects a desired outcome to the set of customer opportunities that could move that outcome, and then branches into possible solutions and assumption tests beneath each opportunity. It moves from abstract to specific as you go down the tree.

### Step 1: Define the Outcome at the Root

Start with a single measurable outcome your team owns. This is the root of the tree. Torres explains the shift it demands: "While as an industry, some companies are moving from this output focus to an outcome focus, most product teams don't really know how to manage this really complex problem of how do I start from an outcome and figure out what to build? It's a really unstructured, wide open, hard problem."

### Step 2: Map the Opportunity Space

Beneath the outcome, map the opportunities — unmet needs, pain points, and desires that customers experience. Torres defines opportunities precisely: "An opportunity is an unmet need, pain point, or desire." She warns that this is where most people fail: "I can tell you that 98% of people that write opportunities write them as solutions."

Structure the opportunity space using an experience map. Torres demonstrates with Netflix: "If you take your outcome — I want to understand what's the experience of somebody using streaming entertainment to entertain themselves. Maybe even broader than Netflix." She walks through the journey: discovering content, evaluating whether it is worth watching, the viewing experience itself, and the post-viewing experience.

The key test: opportunities should contain no solutions. "Whether I work at Netflix or I work at Hulu, our opportunity spaces probably look pretty similar. Now the ones we choose to go after and how we solve them might look really different. But the core human needs of what's your experience as we go about our lives entertaining ourselves is pretty similar."

### Step 3: Move Vertically to Get Specific

As you move down the tree, opportunities get smaller and more actionable. Torres explains why this matters: "As you move vertically down the tree, your opportunities are getting smaller and smaller, which is really key to helping us unlock a continuous cadence. So if I start with that example of I can't decide what to watch, it's a really big hard evergreen problem."

A concrete example: "A really great opportunity in the streaming space is it's hard to enter my password, select specific letters on the screen with the Apple TV remote. If anybody has had an Apple TV, especially the old remote, it's not a very precision device."

### Step 4: Generate Solutions for Each Opportunity

Only after structuring the opportunity space do you brainstorm solutions. Each opportunity can have multiple possible solutions beneath it. The discipline is to stay in the problem space long enough before jumping here.

### Step 5: Define Assumption Tests

Beneath each solution, identify the key assumptions that must be true for the solution to work. Test those assumptions quickly rather than testing the entire idea at once. Torres contrasts this with traditional experimentation: "We have experiments that I actually don't even think we should be running, because they're testing the whole idea before we have any idea if that idea has a strong foundation. They're taking too long. They cost too much money."

She advocates running many small tests: "I'm working with a team that's running half a dozen to a dozen assumption tests in one week, and those assumptions span three ideas. At the end of the week, they can start to compare and contrast those solutions."

## Example

Torres uses Netflix throughout the episode as a running example. The outcome might be "increase engagement." The experience map breaks into phases: discovering something to watch, evaluating options, watching, and post-viewing. Under "discovering," a specific opportunity is "I can't decide what to watch." That branches further into things like "the interface shows me too many options" or "I don't trust the recommendations." Each of those would have distinct solutions and assumption tests beneath them.

Torres notes the difficulty honestly: "I tell my teams when I teach it in class, I say, 'Look, we're going to focus on structuring an opportunity space, and I'm probably going to make you think harder than you've ever had to think in your job,' because we don't think that much at work. We go from meeting to meeting, we stay surface level a lot."

## Output

After applying the Opportunity Solution Tree, you walk away with:

1. **A visual map** connecting your outcome to structured customer opportunities to candidate solutions
2. **A shared language** across the team for why you are building what you are building
3. **A prioritization mechanism** — choose which opportunity to pursue based on size and strategic fit, then compare solutions beneath it
4. **An assumption testing plan** — the fastest, cheapest tests to evaluate each candidate solution
5. **A living document** that evolves as you learn from customer interviews and assumption tests each week
