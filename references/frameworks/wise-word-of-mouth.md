## When to Use

Use this framework when you have a product with real usage and you want to make word-of-mouth your primary growth engine rather than relying on paid acquisition. It is especially relevant for products where the core value is transactional and measurable (fintech, logistics, SaaS tools), where you can identify specific product attributes that drive recommendations. Wise acquired 70% of its million-plus quarterly new users through word of mouth — this is the system behind that number.

## The Framework

Nilan Peiris, CPO of Wise, describes a system that treats word of mouth not as a lucky byproduct but as an engineerable outcome. The core insight: most teams stop building when the product works. To drive recommendations, you have to blow users' socks off.

### Step 1: Measure Word of Mouth Directly

Do not guess. Ask new users how they heard about you.

> "Probably the stat I'm most proud of, and the hardest thing to make happen out of all of that was we acquired 70% of the users that found out about Wise last month through word of mouth. So, contextually, we have 16 million customers, and we're acquiring about a million a quarter."

The measurement method is simple — an interstitial popup asking "How did you hear about us?" at sign-up. Nilan notes this is the same approach Airbnb used.

### Step 2: Identify the Recommendation Drivers

Use NPS surveys and customer feedback to find what attributes drive people to recommend, not just use, the product. At Wise, three things kept surfacing:

> "Customers kept telling us the same things: 'Make it faster, make it cheaper, make it easier to use.'"

The critical distinction is between what makes someone a user and what makes someone an advocate. Most founders stop at "it works." Advocacy requires something much higher:

> "Most founders, and most teams kind of stop when it works. As their next step, some people focus on conversion rate, like, 'I'm going to make this really, really slick,' and that's cool, you get a bit more growth. But to get to recommendation, you're going to blow your users' socks off. You have to give them an experience they didn't know was previously possible."

### Step 3: Set a 10x Bar

Do not aim for incremental improvement. Aim for an order of magnitude better than anything else on the market. This is the threshold for advocacy.

At Wise, that meant:
- **Price**: Dropping fees from 6% down to 0.35%
- **Speed**: Moving money instantly instead of in 3-5 days
- **Ease of use**: Making the entire flow completable in minutes

> "That means figuring out how to move money instantly. That means figuring out how to drop the price all the way from six all the way down to 0.35. And that's because there are systemic infrastructure issues in moving money around the world, which some people haven't solved before."

### Step 4: Engineer Away the Costs

To deliver 10x better, you have to systematically reduce costs at every level. Nilan breaks Wise's P&L into three transaction-level costs: people costs, cost of risk, and partner fees. Then the team engineers each one down.

On risk: "If you're moving a million dollars, you can't usually move it instantly. It might take you two to three days to move it. The rate's locked, could move against us, we'd lose some money." Wise built systems to manage FX exposure algorithmically.

The discipline is to reinvest savings into further improvements: "If you've got this mission of moving the world's money for almost nothing, you've got to invest as much of your cashflow in engineering to try to engineer away these three problems."

### Step 5: Add the Emotional Layer — Mission

Rational product superiority drives recommendations, but emotional alignment amplifies them. Nilan discovered that customers cared deeply about Wise's mission — far more than the team expected.

> "We do our mission, which is to make the world's money move instantly at the touch of a button, for almost nothing. It was a very personal thing, it was like an internal company thing, to think our customers cared about it. And then we rebranded like eight, nine years ago, our first rebrand, and we wrote our mission and sent it to our customers."

Customers started volunteering to help — attending protests, submitting regulatory complaints, sharing Wise's mission on social media. This is not brand marketing in the traditional sense. It is aligning your mission with something customers already believe.

> "There are the rational reasons why people recommend, which we've covered. But there's these emotional ones as well. Softer ones people would call brand. I prefer to call it on the mission."

### Step 6: Close the Perception Gap

Users often do not realize how good the product already is. Making the value visible is itself a growth lever.

Wise found that showing users a graph of how much they saved compared to their bank tripled the sharing rate:

> "Putting that there with this graph, that kind of got me to this definition of product marketing, where customers use the product, and they think they got this value, but when they actually know the value they get — that 3x'd the sharing rate."

Similarly, when Wise achieved instant money transfers, users did not realize the money had arrived until the team added a "wizzy animation" confirming delivery:

> "When you get an instant transfer, there's like this wizzy animation at the end, and you kind of know the money's in the other person's account, ready to spend. And again, you see this big jump in referral rate when that happens, but people need to know it's happened."

Nilan calls this "product marketing within the product" — closing the gap between what you have done and what is perceived to be done.

## Example

Wise started in new markets with NPS scores in the 20s and 30s. Over time, they reached an NPS of 70 — higher than the iPhone and Google Search, in an industry where banks average -30.

The flywheel: lower prices attract more volume, more volume funds engineering that reduces costs further, lower costs enable even lower prices. Meanwhile, each delighted user becomes a recruiter for the next one.

> "And that's what I call a 10x better experience than anyone else in the market, and that led to advocacy and word of mouth off the back of it."

A specific tactical win: Wise enlisted customers to help fight regulatory battles. When regulators in new markets resisted Wise's entry, customers voluntarily submitted complaints and attended public consultations — because they genuinely believed in the mission of cheaper, faster money movement.

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:

1. **A word-of-mouth baseline** — a measured percentage of new users acquired through recommendations
2. **Recommendation drivers** — the specific product attributes that push users from satisfaction to advocacy
3. **A 10x target** — a concrete, quantified goal for each driver (e.g., "instant" not "faster," "0.35%" not "cheaper")
4. **A cost engineering plan** — systematic reduction of the costs that prevent you from delivering 10x value
5. **A perception-closing strategy** — in-product mechanisms that make users aware of the value they are already receiving
6. **A mission alignment** — an emotional reason for users to recruit on your behalf, beyond rational product superiority
