## When to Use

Use this framework when your product is growing but cohort retention curves are declining over time despite no product changes, when you have hit a growth ceiling and need to expand beyond your current user base, or when you are in a hypergrowth phase and the people signing up today are fundamentally different from the people who signed up six months ago. Bangaly developed this framework at Instagram during a period when monthly active users grew from 440 million to 636 million in a single year — 47% growth — and the user base was transforming beneath the team's feet.

> "When we talk to users, first half of 2016, when we talk to women in the US in their 30, they're like, 'Why would I have an Instagram? I have a Facebook account.' Literally people said that, it was that long ago. And then a year later it's like, 'Of course I use Instagram. Instagram is my everything.' The world changed so fast."

## The Framework

### Step 1: Understand Who Your Current Users Are and Why

Before you can identify the adjacent user, you need a clear picture of your existing user base — not just demographics but motivations. What job does the product do for them? Why do they keep coming back?

Bangaly emphasizes what he calls "understand work" — the opposite of building first and justifying later:

> "What I call the anti-pattern of what we want to do. Someone says, 'Hey, you know what? This would be great to build.' And you go pull data to go justify why that would be great to build. Call that identify, justify, execute. First you have to really understand from first principles what is actually going on. So understand, identify, execute."

### Step 2: Identify Who the Adjacent User Is

The adjacent user is the person who could be using your product but for some reason it does not work for them. They are right outside the circle of your current users — not a completely different market, but the next natural cohort.

> "It's really, really important to understand who your users are today and the persona of the user, what motivates them, why they're using it, but then also to understand who is the next user? Who is the user who could be using this product, but for some reason it doesn't work for them."

At Instagram, the team could see adjacent users appearing in the data:

> "One of the ways you start to see the adjacent users starting to adopt the product, especially with the data, is you start seeing cohort curves decline. You start seeing the people who sign up today, three or six months from now, they're signing up and they're doing a worse job. Nothing's changing the product, but just the understanding of how the product should work is different."

### Step 3: Use the Product as the Adjacent User

This is the critical action step. Do not just study the adjacent user from a distance. Actually use your own product the way they would — from a fresh account, on their devices, with their level of familiarity.

> "I try to push my teams to really dog food products in their adjacent user state... a power user who's using a product has... so much history and there's so much informed knowledge on how that product... for that product to actually create a great experience for you that if you were to go and create a new Gmail account and look at YouTube today, I guarantee you it's a completely different and significantly worse experience."

### Step 4: Watch, Talk To, and Visit Adjacent Users

Supplement your own experience by observing real adjacent users interacting with the product:

> "You have to be them, you have to watch how they use the product, you have to talk to them, and then have to visit them and literally see what they're doing in real time in order to make sure that you're enabling the right jobs for them."

### Step 5: Build for Their Jobs to Be Done

The adjacent user often has different jobs to be done than your current power users. At Instacart, Bangaly identified that the product was not built around how people actually shop:

> "When you buy groceries, you're not going to going grocery shopping because you want beautiful ingredients in your fridge. You're going grocery shopping because you actually have a meal to put together. So are we actually reflecting the real job to be done, which is I want to buy tacos, I want to make Taco Tuesday."

He also discovered that reordering — the most common real-world grocery behavior — was buried behind seven or eight clicks:

> "After five times when you go to Instacart, 90% of your order was the same. But when you wanted to reorder, at least back then, you couldn't go and reorder easily."

## Example

At Instagram, the adjacent user shifted constantly during hypergrowth. Early users were tech-savvy early adopters in the US. Then came women in the US who initially saw no reason to have Instagram when they already had Facebook. Then came international users in India and the Philippines with different phones, different connectivity, and different assumptions about how apps should work:

> "They might be less tech savvy. On a scale of an early adopter versus late majority, they might be closer to the late majority. And so we saw this at Instagram. We always knew working on our registration flow, and at one point we were converting at some insanely high percent, and then three months later it would go down by 15%, not because anything was broken, but just because we'd broken into new markets."

At Instacart, the progression was different. The original adjacent user was an office admin ordering for team events. The next was a parent at home with kids who depended on Instacart. Later it was a single person in New York using it for convenience. Each adjacent user optimized for different things and needed different product experiences.

As Lenny summarized: "Basically your growth is going to come from non-existing users. It's the users right outside that circle of... pre-users are today, and you need to think about what do they need that existing users maybe don't need."

## Output

After applying Adjacent User Theory, you walk away with:

1. **A clear profile of your current core user** — who they are, why they use the product, what jobs it does for them
2. **An identified adjacent user persona** — the next cohort just outside your current base, with specific characteristics (tech savviness, geography, use case, device)
3. **A first-hand experience log** — notes from using your own product as the adjacent user, documenting every friction point and broken assumption
4. **A prioritized list of barriers** — the specific product gaps, confusing flows, or missing capabilities that prevent the adjacent user from activating
5. **A roadmap of fixes** — product changes targeted at removing those barriers without degrading the experience for existing users
