## When to Use

Use this when you feel stuck in your career, your manager is not proactively helping you grow, or you do not know what to do to get promoted. The Magic Loop is designed to work even with mediocre or simply busy managers. Ethan Evans, a retired VP at Amazon who led global teams of over 800 and holds 70+ patents, created this framework after seeing it work "like magic" with people at every level, from entry-level engineers to senior directors. It was the sixth most popular post of all time on Lenny's Newsletter across 300+ posts.

## The Framework

The Magic Loop has five steps. They repeat indefinitely.

### Step 1: Do Your Current Job Well

"It's not possible to really grow your career if you're not considered at least performing at a solid level. Now, it doesn't mean you have to be the star on the team at this point, but what you can't have is your boss wishing that you were different."

Evans frames this bluntly: "When you go to your manager and ask, 'What could I do to help?' You don't want their answer, even if they don't say it quite so bluntly, to be, 'Do your F-ing job.' You need to be doing that already."

Important nuance: "A good job is in the eyes of your manager in this case. You may think I'm doing great work, but if your manager doesn't, they're the ones you need to build as an ally here."

### Step 2: Ask Your Manager How You Can Help

"Speaking as a manager, and I've talked to hundreds of managers, very few people go and ask their manager, 'What can I do to help you? What do you need?' And so just asking sets you apart, and it begins to build a relationship that we're on the same team, that I'm here as a part of your organization to make you successful, not just myself."

### Step 3: Do What They Ask

"Whatever they say, do it. So you dig a big hole. If you say, 'What could I do to help you?' And they say, 'Well, we really need someone to take out the tray sheets today,' and you're like, 'Oh, I didn't mean that. I wanted exciting work.' So do what they ask, help out even if it's not your favorite work."

### Step 4: Connect Their Needs to Your Goals

This is where the magic happens. Go back to your manager and say: "Hey, I'm really enjoying working with you. I'm wondering is there some way I could help you that would also help me reach my goal?"

Evans explains: "Whether that goal is to change roles or get a raise or get a promotion, you say, 'My goal is I'd really like to learn this new skill. Is there something you need that would also help me learn this new skill?' And the reason this works is managers help those who help them. It's just human nature."

For this to work, you have to know what your goal is. "You have to be clear on what it is you want. Well, that's part's up to you."

### Step 5: Repeat

"Like lather, rinse, repeat with your shampoo. Step five is once you're working with your manager towards your goal and discussing where you're going, and you're helping each other, the magic of the loop is just go around and around."

Over time, you build trust and can evolve from asking to suggesting to acting: "You go from asking your manager, 'How can I help?' To suggesting to your manager, 'These are some things I see that seem like they need to be done. Would you like me to do them?' To just seeing what needs to be done and sort of keeping your leader in the loop and saying, 'Hey, I noticed that we have this problem. I fixed it.'"

Evans calls this "advanced mode" and warns: "Don't jump straight to this. Don't just start suggesting things, because you may get it wrong."

### Why It Works

"First, I mentioned how rare it is for managers to be offered help. If you're a manager, you'll recognize this... Management can be a lonely job, because you feel like you're responsible for everything. So having an ally, it's just a huge weight off people's shoulders."

"And then I think a lot about social engineering. The social engineering here is just the simple, 'You help me, I'll help you.' It doesn't have to be exploitative, it's just we help those people who help us, and that's built into human survival."

### Common Objection: "Shouldn't My Manager Do This?"

Evans addresses this head-on: "What your manager should do and $4 will get you a cup of coffee at Starbucks. The point of this loop is it's in your control."

"Not all managers are good and some of them need some help... You can be upset that your manager isn't perfect, but move on from that and take control of your own situation."

### For Managers: Run the Loop in Reverse

"If you are a manager or a leader of any type, you can initiate The Magic Loop from your side, so you can talk to your employees and say, 'Hey, what are your career goals? Would you like to form a partnership where you step up to new challenges and I help you get to your goals?'"

Evans reports: "I had a lot of success forming this kind of partnership with my employees, where as they saw growth and success, they really leaned in and like, 'This system works. You're actually investing in me now. I'll work extra hard.'"

## Example

Evans shares two stories from opposite ends of the spectrum. An entry-level person wrote to him: "A year ago I was made redundant. I got this first job and I got an offer for an increased salary, and then I got the second job and I got an increase when I joined that was even bigger." He was in the position of needing to slow down and digest all the progress.

On the executive end, one of Evans's best people at Amazon "joined my team as what we would call an SDE II, which in Amazon is a level five employee." Over eight years following this process, "He went from a mid-level engineer to an executive with a team of 800 people." He eventually founded his own startup, sold it, and now works as an EVP at a major online bank.

## Output

After applying this framework, you walk away with:
- Clarity on whether you are doing your current job well enough to start the loop (step 1 check)
- A specific ask to bring to your next one-on-one: "What can I do to help you?"
- A defined career goal to connect to your manager's needs in step 4
- A repeating cadence of help-ask-connect that compounds over months and years
