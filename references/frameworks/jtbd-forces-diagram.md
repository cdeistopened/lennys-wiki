## When to Use

Use the Forces of Progress diagram when you have a product that should be winning on features but customers are not switching. It applies when you are trying to understand why a clearly better solution is not getting adopted, when you need to diagnose where in the decision process customers get stuck, or when your team keeps adding features thinking that more pull will drive conversion. Moesta frames the fundamental problem: "Most people say, 'If I just add more features, create more pull, people will buy.' It's not true. More features create actually anxiety — can it do all those things?"

## The Framework

The Forces of Progress diagram maps four forces that act on every customer considering a switch from an old product (or no product) to a new one. Two forces push toward change; two forces resist it. A customer only moves when the driving forces exceed the resisting forces.

### Force 1 (F1): Push of the Current Situation

This is the context that makes the customer dissatisfied with their current state. It has nothing to do with your new product — it exists independently. Moesta explains: "The push or the context they're in has nothing to do with the new product, it's the only reason why they would leave the old product. And if there's no push, they can't even see your product because we're creatures of habit."

Without a push, no amount of marketing or features will matter. The customer is not even in the market.

### Force 2 (F2): Pull of the New Solution

This is the magnetism of a better outcome — the vision of what life could look like with the new product. Moesta describes how F1 and F2 work together: "I have to be in this situation and I have to want this outcome. As soon as I have a push and I have some idea of what's possible, then I create F2, which is basically the pull to a new outcome, a new state, a new thing."

### Force 3 (F3): Anxiety of the New

Every new product creates uncertainty. Will it work? Can I learn it? What if I make the wrong choice? Moesta calls this out as the force most teams ignore: "Every time I show somebody something new, it actually creates anxiety. And I call this F3." This is why adding features can backfire — each additional feature is another thing the customer wonders whether they can actually use.

### Force 4 (F4): Habit of the Present

Even when customers are dissatisfied, they cling to what they know. The old way is familiar, predictable, and requires no effort. Moesta describes F4 as the force that keeps people stuck even when they complain: the phrase he uses is "Bitchin' ain't switchin'. Just because people bitch about something doesn't mean they're going to do anything about it."

### The Equation

**F1 + F2 > F3 + F4 = Customer switches.**

Moesta states the rule directly: "If F1 and F2 are not greater than F3 and F4, they're not going to move, they're not going to do anything."

### The Key Insight: Reduce Friction Instead of Adding Features

Most teams only work on F2 — making the product more attractive. Moesta argues that reducing F3 and F4 (the bottom forces) is often more effective and cheaper: "If I reduce friction, which is the bottom part, I actually don't have to do anything with a product, I just have to make it easier."

## Example

Moesta built condos for downsizers — people moving from 3,000 square foot homes to 1,500 square foot condos. He kept losing sales when buyers would cancel six weeks after signing. The reason was not the condo (F2 was strong). The reason was F4 — the habit of the present, specifically all their stuff: "They'd cancel six weeks later because they didn't know how to get rid of all their stuff, which is a frictional point."

His solution attacked F3 and F4, not F2: "I actually raised the price of the condo, included moving and two years of storage as part of the deal with the condo because it's the frictional coefficient and I increased sales over 30%."

In a separate example, he shares how the dining room table became the emotional blocker for elderly downsizers. People said they did not want a dining room. But if they did not know where their dining room table would go, they would not move. The table represented "the emotional bank account for their entire life." Moesta sacrificed the second bedroom suite to add space for the table — a solution no survey would have surfaced — and increased sales 22%.

## Output

After applying the Forces of Progress diagram, you walk away with:

1. **A diagnostic map** of the four forces acting on your specific customer segment
2. **Clarity on where to intervene** — most teams over-invest in F2 (features/pull) and under-invest in reducing F3 (anxiety) and F4 (habit)
3. **Specific friction points** to remove from your onboarding, sales process, or product experience
4. **A framework for evaluating competitive threats** — your real competitors are whatever the customer would do instead, which may not be another product in your category
5. **Interview questions** oriented around pushes, pulls, anxieties, and habits rather than feature preferences
