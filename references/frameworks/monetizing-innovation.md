## When to Use

Use this framework when you are building a new product or feature and need to determine pricing strategy before committing engineering resources. It applies at two stages: the startup phase (getting your first pricing right) and the scale-up phase (extracting full value from every deal). It is especially critical for AI companies where value capture from day one determines whether you build a durable business or train customers to expect more for less.

## The Framework

Madhavan Ramanujam's core thesis across both *Monetizing Innovation* and *Scaling Innovation* is that founders must master two engines simultaneously — market share and wallet share — and that pricing is the connective tissue between them.

> "If you want to build an enduring business, you need to be able to architect towards profitable growth. What that means is you need to be able to master two engines: market share and wallet share."

> "If you're flying an aircraft, you don't want to fly on one engine. Why do you actually want to do that for your business?"

### Step 1: Identify Your Archetype and Avoid Its Traps

Ramanujam identifies three founder archetypes, each with two common traps:

**The Disruptor** (acquisition-focused):
- Trap 1: You land but cannot expand — you gave the farm away upfront with nothing to upsell.
- Trap 2: You win market share but cannot hold it — you keep acquiring but neglect existing customers.

**The Moneymaker** (monetization-focused):
- Trap 1: You nickel and dime customers to death with hidden fees and complex tiers.
- Trap 2: The price premium paradox — pricing so high it kills acquisition entirely.

**The Community Builder** (retention-focused):
- Trap 1: So focused on your loyal base that you miss the frontier and stop acquiring new types of customers.
- Trap 2: You train your best customers to expect more for less.

> "Being a profitable growth architect means that you're simultaneously being a disruptor, a moneymaker, and a community builder all at the same time."

### Step 2: Start with Beautifully Simple Pricing

In the startup phase, your pricing must pass one test: can your customers articulate it back to you?

> "Take some of your early prospects or customers and ask them to articulate the pricing strategy back to you. If they cannot contextualize that in a simple manner and actually explain, you don't have a simple pricing strategy. It's as simple as that."

Your pricing also needs to tell a value story — contextualizing the price against the outcome.

> "Superhuman came up with a $30 price point per month, which was pretty simple. But the way they told the story was that you pay a dollar a day for actually getting four hours of productivity back in the week. It's like the price for a latte in a week to actually get four hours back. Why wouldn't I actually do that?"

### Step 3: Co-Create the Business Case Through POCs

Frame every proof-of-concept not as a technical test but as a business case exercise. Agree on inputs with the customer from day one so they cannot challenge the output.

> "The POC should be framed as the entire goal of the POC is to create a business case, period, full stop. It is not to demonstrate product functionality fit within your customer environment."

> "If you have done that process and the customer agrees on all the inputs, they're very unlikely to push back on the output of an ROI model."

Build the ROI model around three buckets: incremental gains (KPI improvements), cost savings (headcount, licenses), and opportunity cost (what your team does with the time saved).

### Step 4: Master Negotiations with Gives, Gets, and Options

At the scale-up phase, pricing strategy means nothing if you cannot defend it in a conversation.

**Gives and Gets:** Never give a concession without asking for something in return. One powerful get: the value audit — every six months, the customer's team conducts an internal value assessment of your product.

> "If they actually engage in that, that gives you tremendous pricing power for renegotiations because it is their business case, they've championed it internally, and you're pretty much making your products pretty sticky."

**Show Up with Options:** Never present one product at one price. Offer good-better-best to shift the conversation from price to value.

> "If your customer is budget conscious, they'll say, 'Hey, I like the hundred K price point, but I actually like the functionality in your 200K product.' Then your immediate question is, what in the functionality do you actually like? So you switch the conversation back to value."

**Taper Your Concessions:** Start with your largest discount and decrease each time.

> "The best negotiators taper the concessions. They would say, 'I can give you 15%.' 'Okay, I need more.' 'I'll give you five.' 'I need more.' 'I'll give you two.' So you're automatically indicating to the other person that the negotiation's actually ending."

### Step 5: For AI Companies — Tackle Monetization from Day One

AI pricing differs because value is attributable and costs are real from the start.

> "The winners in AI will need to master monetization from day one. If you're bringing a lot of value to the table and you start at training your customers to expect $20 a month and you anchored yourself on a low price point, you're in trouble."

> "20% of what you build drives 80% of the willingness to pay. But the irony is that that 20% is the easiest thing to build often."

## Example

Ramanujam coached a founder who believed the customer budget was $100K but whose product delivered $500K in value. Rather than either accepting the low anchor or nervously pitching the high number, Ramanujam advised presenting two pricing model options: $100K base plus 10% of incremental value delivered, or $500K fixed.

> "Either the customer says, 'That's great. You're putting skin in the game. Let's go with a hundred K and 10%,' or, 80% of the situations, you might actually want to avoid the outcome-based pricing as a buyer, but you're not really fixated on the 500K at that point. It is the premium that you're actually paying for the certainty."

The founder closed at $400K — a 4X improvement over where they would have landed with a single price point.

Superhuman is the startup-phase example: competing against free email products with a $30/month premium, justified by the value story of "a dollar a day for four hours of productivity back."

## Output

After applying this framework, you should have:

1. **A clear archetype diagnosis** — which traps you are most likely falling into, and which engine you are neglecting
2. **A pricing story** your customers can repeat back to you in one sentence
3. **A POC structure** framed around co-creating a business case, not testing technical functionality
4. **An ROI model** built on customer-validated inputs across incremental gains, cost savings, and opportunity cost
5. **A negotiation playbook** with options, gives/gets, and tapering concession strategy
6. **For AI founders:** a day-one monetization model that captures value proportional to the outcome delivered
