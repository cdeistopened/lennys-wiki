# Bowling Pin Strategy

## When to Use

Use this when deciding whether to go broad or go niche, when considering category creation, or when your startup is trying to compete with an established market leader. The bowling pin strategy -- originally from Geoffrey Moore's work on technology adoption -- is the pattern April Dunford says "almost all successful companies follow." It is the counter-argument to the popular advice that you must create a new category from day one to build a legendary business.

## The Framework

### The Core Idea

Instead of attacking a large market head-on, define a small segment that the market leader underserves. Dominate that segment. Then use that beachhead to expand into adjacent segments, knocking them over like bowling pins until you are large enough to challenge the incumbent.

> "The easiest way to take over a great big market is to define a segment of the market that is underserved by the market leader." -- April Dunford

### Step 1: Identify the Lead Pin

Find a subsegment of an existing market where the dominant player either does not care about the customers or actively underserves them. This is your beachhead -- the one pin you must knock over first.

> "If you think about a bunch of bowling pins, we're going to circle around the lead pin, which is this little piece of the market that's easy for us to go get, and the market leader probably doesn't care about it." -- April Dunford

### Step 2: Dominate the Lead Pin

Position yourself as the best solution for this specific segment. Use an existing market category with a modifier (e.g., "CRM for investment banks") so you do not have to explain what the category is -- only who you serve within it.

> "If I'm positioning in an existing category, I can say, 'Well, I'm CRM for very small businesses.' That was Salesforce's initial positioning. I didn't have to explain to you what a CRM was, that already existed." -- April Dunford

### Step 3: Expand to Adjacent Pins

Once you dominate the lead pin, expand into the two or three segments immediately adjacent. Each pin you knock over makes the next ones easier.

### Step 4: Challenge the Market Leader

After enough adjacent segments, you have the scale, brand recognition, and product maturity to compete for the whole market.

### Step 5 (Optional): Create a Category

Only after you dominate significant market share should you consider redefining the category. This is where category creation actually makes strategic sense -- not at the start.

## Example

**April Dunford's CRM company** in the early 2000s provides the textbook bowling pin illustration:

> "I worked at this company, we thought we were enterprise software. We narrowed it down to CRM for investment banks, and so we literally drew bowling pins for our investors and said, 'Lead pin is CRM for investment banks. Once we knock that over, then we're going to go to retail banking, then we're going to be CRM for banking. And then once we knock that over, we're going to go to insurance, then we're going to be CRM for financial services. Once we knock that over, then we're going to be a great big company and we're going to challenge Siebel to be CRM for enterprise software.' Instead we got acquired by Siebel for like $1 billion, but whatever." -- April Dunford

**Salesforce** followed the same pattern. Initial positioning: CRM for very small businesses (no IT department needed because there is no software to install). They were not creating a new category. They were a cloud CRM for a specific underserved segment. Category creation (the broader "cloud" movement) came later.

**Qualtrics** was survey software until they reached $300M in revenue dominating that existing category, then repositioned as "customer experience software."

**Snowflake** was "data warehousing for the cloud" -- an existing market with a cloud modifier -- until they were approaching their IPO, then expanded to "cloud data" to capture data lakes and broader data infrastructure.

> "If we just fast forwarded to when they were 300 million, we can say 'Category creation's the only thing to do,' but it's like, well, what happened before then? That was something different." -- April Dunford

## Why Not Category Creation First?

Dunford is not against category creation. She is against doing it too early:

> "I think category creation is, again, it's good, it works, but I think we have to be very careful when we're thinking about doing that at an early stage business or a business that isn't already dominating the category that they're in." -- April Dunford

The risk: you do the hard work of educating the market on a new category, and at the exact moment it gains traction, a well-funded fast follower learns from your mistakes and overtakes you.

> "A more common thing that happens is that the company does the hard work of creating the category and in exactly the moment where the category starts to take hold, fast follower can come in with fresh funding and a lot of it and learn from all your mistakes and come in and knock you off the peak right when you think you've made it." -- April Dunford

She also notes that most category creators do not actually win long term: "What happened to MySpace? What happened to Ask Jeeves? Most category creators actually get overtaken by fast followers."

## Output

After applying this framework you walk away with:

- Your lead pin: a specific underserved segment you can dominate
- A positioning statement that uses an existing category + segment modifier
- A visual bowling pin map showing 4-6 pins in sequence from niche to broad
- A timeline: dominate lead pin first, then expand
- Clear criteria for when (and whether) to pursue category creation
