## When to Use

Use this as a pre-flight checklist before approving any growth initiative. It is especially valuable when a new head of growth joins the company, when a growth team is planning its quarterly roadmap, or when leadership is pressuring the team for quick wins. If any item on this list appears on your roadmap as a growth driver, you should either reframe the initiative or cut it.

## The Framework

Elena Verna compiled this list from patterns she has seen repeatedly across her work at Miro, Amplitude, Dropbox, SurveyMonkey, and dozens of advisory engagements. Each tactic is something companies invest significant resources into expecting growth — and it consistently fails to deliver.

> "Growth is a fairly new field. You have a lot of renowned interest in growth hacks, like what is the sure things to get growth. Oftentimes things that are completely out of context or they are very specific to one example and actually do not apply as a pattern."

### 1. Hiring a Head of Growth Too Early

Do not hire a growth team until you have product-market fit and enough data to experiment on. Founder-led growth should carry you to at least $1M ARR, often much further.

> "In order to get the growth going in the company, a founder and the founding team have to figure out how to make it grow to the first million, 5 million, 10 million in ARR. To figure out your product market fit and how to distribute it, it's not something that you can outsource to somebody."

> "Honestly, the longer you wait, the better it is because that way your entire company will be trained to be responsible for growth as opposed to putting this one island with a growth team."

### 2. Hiring a Head of Growth to Reverse Decline

If your business is slowing down, a growth hire will not fix it. Growth teams amplify momentum — they do not create it.

> "If you have the overall business slowing down, your head of growth is destined to fail because the reason business is slowing down is much deeper than not having a growth team."

> "Growth can amplify great product market fit and growth can help you grow faster once you are already growing. But if you are slowing down and you have issues with either your go-to-market strategy or your core product strategy, growth is going to be absolutely helpless."

### 3. Rebranding or Redesigning Your Homepage to Drive Growth

Every time a new CMO arrives and redesigns the marketing site, performance drops. Rebrands are a step backward that require months of optimization just to reach the previous baseline.

> "Never ever once have I seen a rebrand or redesign, especially of a marketing site, produce good performance results."

> "Every single time I've seen a marketing site rebrand, it's been a step back in performance that then is being treated as a fire drill to fix."

This applies to product redesigns too:

> "Product redesign. How much product and engineering work comes into just changing a logo in your product or changing colors? It's insane. With no results ever, there's no results."

### 4. Copying the Competition

Use competitors for inspiration and pattern recognition, but copying their flows directly fails 95% of the time because you do not know what variant you are seeing, what data drove their decisions, or whether it even works for them.

> "Copying competition is like the fastest way to mediocrity because you'll never be a leader if you copied somebody else."

### 5. Treating Every Problem as Unique

Conversely, do not ignore patterns. Most growth problems have been solved before. Hire advisors, study cross-industry patterns, and invest in learning.

> "Solving any one problem uniquely is extremely inefficient. We are evolving so fast in our market, you need to be able to patternize your solutions."

### 6. Growth Teams Prioritizing Paid and SEO Over Earned Channels

Paid acquisition makes Google richer. SEO depends on an algorithm that can change overnight. Earned channels — virality, word of mouth, user-generated content — are yours forever.

> "If you build your own user-generated content in your own community, nobody else can compete with you in that. That is yours. Your competitors cannot buy their eyeballs."

> "Focusing on these earned channels that you own becomes the outmost priority. And if you don't have them on your growth roadmap, you are going to be in some really big trouble over the next year to two years."

Verna cites Dropbox: over 50% of acquisition comes through the sharing loop — a stable earned channel that nobody can compete with.

### 7. Failing to Layer New Growth Models

No single growth loop lasts forever. Every 18 months you should be introducing a new growth initiative, and every 5 years something big needs to take a major share of your volume.

> "Most of growth loops spin out their ability to produce meaningful results for you within the first five to six to seven years."

Dedicate 20-25% of the growth team's time annually to exploring new loops. Expect most to fail — that is fine.

> "It took us probably 18 months until we started putting metrics expectations on it. Before that, it was a thing that we were testing."

### 8. Not Hiring Advisors

Growth teams that try to figure everything out on their own are underperforming. One hour per week from someone who has solved your problem at another company is the highest-leverage investment you can make.

> "Any growth team that does not have an advisor is a growth team that is underperforming."

Verna's vetting tip: do a paid workshop on a real problem before committing to a retainer. See the advisor in action.

### 9. Testing Everything

If every initiative on your growth roadmap is an A/B test, you are paralyzed. Trust intuition for low-traffic surfaces. Use pre/post analysis instead.

> "If every single one of your initiatives that you're doing on growth is an experiment, that's a problem. It's almost like a disease, like a paralyzing disease."

> "My rule of thumb, if we cannot collect the sample size in the month, we shouldn't test it, period."

### 10. The Fire Round (Four Micro Anti-Patterns)

**Color optimizations:** "A blue is a blue is a blue. As long as it's accessible and as long as it's bright enough, off you go. That's an early 2000s tactic."

**Third-party signups as growth drivers:** Adding Google Auth will not increase acquisition. It is a UX feature, not a growth tactic.

**One-email wonders:** A single email will never move a metric. Email is a strategy, not a tactic. "Please don't stress about one-off email. Never test a change in one email. It'll never work."

**Simplifying for simplification's sake:** Removing steps from onboarding is an action, not a problem. The problem is confusion, cognitive load, or lack of education. Simplification is only a solution if that is the actual diagnosis.

> "If you ever have a line item on your roadmap that says simplified onboarding, please cross it out. It's not going to work because simplifying onboarding is an action. What is the problem that you're solving?"

## Example

Verna describes the Mirrorverse at Miro — a user-generated content library of boards people created — as an example of investing in earned channels. It took 18 months before the team put metrics expectations on it. Eventually it became both an engagement engine and an acquisition engine.

At Dropbox, the sharing loop accounts for 50% of acquisition and has been firing for 17 years — an earned channel no competitor can replicate.

The recurring counterexample: new CMOs arriving and redesigning the marketing site, promising acquisition lifts. The result, every time: performance drops, followed by a fire drill to recover, followed by 6+ months of optimization to merely return to baseline.

## Output

After running your growth roadmap through this checklist, you should have:

1. **A clean roadmap** — any initiative that matches these anti-patterns is either reframed with realistic expectations or removed
2. **An earned channel investment** — at least one initiative focused on virality, UGC, or word-of-mouth
3. **A growth model layering plan** — what new loop are you introducing this 18-month window?
4. **An advisor on retainer** — or at minimum, a workshop scheduled with a candidate
5. **A testing policy** — which surfaces get A/B tests, which get pre/post, and which get shipped on intuition

Verna summarizes her philosophy:

> "Those frameworks are like my church in my mind of my system of beliefs of how I think about growth and how I think about it on a strategic level of owning it as a strategy, not just like a tactic or an initiative."
